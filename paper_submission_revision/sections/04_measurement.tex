The experiments answer five questions in turn. Does score-then-select reduce
the computation that is executed (Q1, Section~\ref{sec:composed})? Does the
reduced computation become reduced latency (Q2, Section~\ref{sec:latency})?
When can a fused score-then-select kernel be faster than dense attention, and
where does its time go (Q3, Section~\ref{sec:fused})? Does the answer depend on
numerical precision (Q4, Section~\ref{sec:precision})? And what changes when
peers are selected before scoring (Q5, Section~\ref{sec:prescore})?

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We time the attention core --- from queries, keys and values to the message
tensor --- because the input and output projections are shared by every
implementation and dilute the quantity in question. Unless stated otherwise the
configuration is fp32, head width $d{=}16$, batch $256$, on one NVIDIA
Blackwell GPU (sm\_120) under PyTorch 2.11, CUDA 12.8 and Triton 3.6. The dense
baseline is the fastest of PyTorch's fused scaled-dot-product path and a
streaming dense kernel we wrote in Triton and tuned over the same parameter
space and admissibility filter as our sparse kernels; Q4 adds FlexAttention
\citep{dong2025flexattention}. Every ratio in this paper is against that best
dense implementation, and a ratio below one means the sparse path is faster.

Timing uses on-device CUDA events with explicit synchronisation. The device was
shared with other tenants and clocks could not be locked, so implementations
are timed \emph{interleaved}, repetition by repetition, within one sweep per
agent count; interference then lands on all of them together and their ratios
stay paired. The primary statistic is the minimum over samples, an estimate of the uncontended cost; the whole block is repeated three times and we report where the ratio of medians changes a conclusion. Recomputing every comparison from medians of the same samples changes which implementation is faster at one of $24$
configurations in the main sweep, and reverses the end-to-end pre-score result
of Q5 in one of its two sweeps; both are stated where they occur. Agent counts
below $192$ are launch-bound on this device and carry no claim. Every
implementation is checked against a reference at every configuration whose
latency is reported, and all agree to fp32 rounding except at exact ties at the
$k$-th score (Appendix~\ref{app:verification}). Appendix~\ref{sec:protocol}
gives the full protocol, including the tiling search, and
Appendix~\ref{sec:pitfalls} the errors it guards against.

\subsection{Q1: Is the reduction executed?}
\label{sec:composed}

Only in some implementations, and the count cannot tell which. PyTorch's FLOP
counter, attached to the running program, confirms that the gathered
implementation executes the reduction Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} permits.
The masked implementation multiplies the discarded entries as zeros and so
executes dense arithmetic plus selection, while a count based on the retained
fraction credits it with a $75\%$ saving at $N{=}512$, $k{=}127$.

Not in this regime. Composed from general-purpose operators, the gathered path
executes less arithmetic than dense attention and is slower than every dense
implementation at every agent count we measure, by up to a factor of
thirty-seven at $512$ agents and a quarter of peers retained
(Table~\ref{tab:headline}).

The byte criterion of Section~\ref{sec:roofline} accounts for this: at $d{=}16$ and $N{=}512$ the quarter-of-peers operating point is four times past its leading-order bound. Timed in
isolation, the sparse contraction and the dense multiply it replaces both run
near the bandwidth the device attains on a large copy, so neither is on a worse
hardware path; the sparse one is slower because it moves about four times the
bytes. The largest single stage of the composed path is selection, which runs at under a tenth of attainable bandwidth (Appendix~\ref{app:stages}).

These are attention-stage measurements, and two wider views shrink them. The
ordering survives the backward pass with a narrower margin --- at $512$ agents
the gathered path is $18.4$ times the fused dense path on the forward pass and
$6.2$ times on forward and backward, since selection has almost no adjoint ---
and in a complete MAPPO iteration at six to twenty-four agents the environment
and per-step action selection take $78$ to $96\%$ of the time and all implementations are within $2\%$ of
one another (Appendix~\ref{app:backward}). Neither a speedup nor a slowdown of
the attention stage is an end-to-end speedup or slowdown in that pipeline.

\subsection{Q3: When is a fused score-then-select kernel faster, and why so rarely?}
\label{sec:fused}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering

saving at every sparsity. Shading marks the region in which sparsity is

\label{fig:ksweep}
\end{figure}

The composed path spends most of its time moving intermediates that a fused
kernel need not create. We wrote fused kernels that hold the score row on chip
and never write a score matrix, an index tensor or a gathered value tensor to
memory. At $512$ agents retaining a single peer the composed sparse path costs
$4.62$\,ms and the fused one $0.139$\,ms, a factor of $33$ attributable to the
implementation and not to the algorithm, and the fused path is then faster than
the best dense implementation (Figure~\ref{fig:ksweep}).

Matched authorship is a weaker condition than matched efficiency, so we searched
both kernels over one parameter space --- query tile, key tile or head chunk,
warp count and pipeline depth --- under one shared-memory admissibility filter,
and report the fraction of the device's matrix-multiply rate each reaches
(Table~\ref{tab:autotune}, Appendix~\ref{app:selection}). At head widths $16$
and $32$ the fused sparse kernel is the \emph{less} efficient of the two at
every agent count --- $21$ to $33\%$ of the device's rate against $30$ to $47\%$
for dense --- and it is faster at one retained peer only because it performs
about half the arithmetic. The search is not equal: the sparse kernel must hold
an $N$-wide score tile, so above $192$ agents the filter admits three sparse
configurations against $28$ to $106$ dense ones. The sparse figures are
therefore lower bounds, the negative entries at head width $64$ and above are
weakly supported, and the positive entry is conservative. We call the sparse
kernel the best implementation we found under these constraints, not an optimal
one.

The advantage is narrow. It appears at one retained peer, and at two only at
$d{=}32$, $N{=}512$; the $k{=}1$ case is degenerate as attention, since a
softmax over one element is identically one and the mechanism reduces to copying
the highest-scoring peer's value. At four retained peers the fused kernel is $1.3$ to $2.3$ times slower than dense, and at a quarter of peers more than an
order of magnitude slower. Three ways of selecting inside the kernel ---
repeated argmax, bisection on the score's bit pattern, and one sort of
score--index pairs --- trade a cost linear in $k$ for dense aggregation or for
a sort that costs five to sixteen times dense attention, and none changes the
outcome (Appendix~\ref{app:selection}).

To see where the fused kernel's time goes, we held the retained set fixed and
changed only how it is found (Table~\ref{tab:fixedset}). Given the exact top-$k$
indices for free, a fused kernel that reads one key and one value row per
retained peer costs $0.06$ to $0.11$ times dense attention at $512$ agents and
$k \le 4$; the fused score-then-select kernel, attending over the same peers,
costs $0.77$ to $1.93$ times. From $384$ agents upward, at least $90\%$ of the
fused kernel's device time is the cost of finding the set, which is the term
Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} says cannot be removed. Finding the same set
with library operators instead, a score product followed by \texttt{torch.topk},
costs $8$ to $27$ times dense attention on its own at $d{=}16$. This sweep ran on the
other card of the host from the main sweep, and the fused kernel's ratios
reproduce it ($0.77$, $1.27$ and $1.93$ against $0.81$, $1.27$ and $1.96$ at
$d{=}16$, $N{=}512$, $k \in \{1,2,4\}$). What is fundamental to exact
score-then-select is that all $N$ scores are \emph{computed}; that our kernels
also hold them \emph{resident} is a property of our implementations, and a streaming exact top-$k$, such as the chunked selection of \citet{gupta2021topk}, might find the set faster.

\subsection{Q4: Does the crossover depend on precision?}
\label{sec:precision}

The single-peer advantage above is measured in fp32 with IEEE products, which
keeps every kernel off the tensor cores so that it reproduces the reference
summation order. That is the format in which dense attention is least
advantaged. We repeated the comparison in TF32 and bf16, searching both kernels
again in each format under the same admissibility filter, and added
FlexAttention \citep{dong2025flexattention} twice: as a dense baseline, and as
PyTorch's route to data-dependent sparsity, with the top-$k$ retained set
expressed as a block mask (Table~\ref{tab:precision}).

Tensor cores help dense attention far more than they help the fused top-$k$
kernel. The best dense implementation becomes $2.7$ to $4.0$ times faster in
TF32 than in fp32, and $3.9$ to $8.1$ times faster in bf16. The fused kernel at
one retained peer, faster than dense at every agent count in fp32 ($0.67$ to
$0.91$), is slower from $384$ agents upward in both reduced formats ($1.07$ to
$1.38$) and at most marginally faster at $192$ agents ($0.83$ to $1.01$). One reduced-precision cell is at parity rather than slower under the ratio of medians ($0.99$, bf16, $d{=}16$, $N{=}512$). This
separates the two readings that the fp32 data could not: at a fixed head width,
raising the efficiency of dense attention removes the crossover, so it depends
on how close dense attention runs to the machine rather than on the head width
as such. TF32 also changes the function computed. Rounding the products
reorders near-tied scores, so every top-$k$ implementation --- fused, composed or
FlexAttention --- retains a different peer from a float64 reference in up to
$0.7\%$ of query rows, rising with $k$; we exclude configurations above
$0.1\%$, which removes most TF32 entries beyond one retained peer, and since
cost rises with $k$ those entries could only be slower.

FlexAttention is the fastest dense implementation in nine of the twelve
reduced-precision configurations, which supports the choice of baseline. As a
route to top-$k$ sparsity it is $8$ to $218$ times slower than dense attention:
the retained set must first be ranked from a full score matrix and converted
into a block mask on every call, and at $128$-wide blocks every block of the
mask contains a retained pair, so the kernel then computes densely.

\subsection{Q5: Selecting before scoring}
\label{sec:prescore}

We test Proposition~\ref{prop:escape} with three selectors, each used by
existing multi-agent mechanisms and each in a different class of
Corollary~\ref{cor:taxonomy}. \emph{All-pairs nearest neighbours} ranks the
distances between two-dimensional positions, as a neighbourhood rule
recomputed each step does \citep{jiang2018dgn,jiang2018atoc}; it is case~(b),
and its count ratio rises towards $d$ and no further. \emph{Grid nearest
neighbours} buckets agents in a uniform grid and ranks only the candidates in the surrounding $3 \times 3$ cells; its cost is $\Theta(Nk)$ plus one sort of the agents, case~(d) up to a sort that is under $5\%$ of its count here, and it retains the exact nearest neighbours in at least $99.93\%$ of
cases for uniformly placed agents. \emph{Z-order} orders agents along a Morton curve and keeps the $k$ adjacent in that order, as ZETA does for low-dimensional key projections in sequence models \citep{zeng2025zeta}; its sort is $O(N \log N)$, case~(c). Closed forms are in Appendix~\ref{app:closedforms}. Attention then
runs over the retained pairs only, composed from library operators or as the
fused fixed-set kernel of Q3; the Z-order window, a band in curve order, also
runs through FlexAttention. These selectors attend to different peers than
top-$k$, so this comparison is of cost, not of quality; Table~\ref{tab:fixedset}
is the comparison at a fixed set.

\paragraph{The attention stage.} Once the retained set is known, the fused
attention over it behaves as the accounting predicts: at two retained peers it
costs $0.06$ times dense attention at $512$ agents, more than sixteen times
faster, and its advantage grows with the agent count because the score term now shrinks with the aggregation (Figure~\ref{fig:prescore} in Appendix~\ref{app:prescore}). The ratio of medians is $0.064$ in that sweep but $0.18$ in the second, more contended one, and under CUDA-graph replay the same kernel over the exact top-$k$ set costs $0.07$ (Table~\ref{tab:fixedset}). The figure is a device time: the kernel runs for about ten microseconds, so an eager deployment whose
host launch path is slower than that will not see it in full. Composed from
library operators the same stage is slower than dense attention at every agent
count and sparsity measured, and fusing it is worth a factor of $4$ to $17$
(Appendix~\ref{app:prescore}). No score-then-select implementation comes close,
because none can remove the score term.

\paragraph{End to end, the selector decides.} The three selectors separate as
Corollary~\ref{cor:taxonomy} predicts, and by more than their operation counts
suggest (Table~\ref{tab:selectors}). The all-pairs selector's cost grows
quadratically, from $0.32$ to $5.69$\,ms between $192$ and $512$ agents at four
retained peers, and its path ends $37$ times slower than dense attention; the
cap binds far below its analytic value because the selector's cost is traffic
rather than arithmetic. The grid selector removes the quadratic growth --- its
cost rises from $0.51$ to $1.67$\,ms over the same range --- but its constant,
gathering and ranking about a hundred candidates per agent, keeps the path $10$
times slower than dense at $512$ agents, and at sixteen retained peers it is
slower than the all-pairs selector at $384$ and $512$ agents: within the
measured range the asymptotic class does not fix the ordering. The Z-order
selector's cost is nearly flat, $0.10$ to $0.14$\,ms, so its share falls as
attention grows more expensive: the path costs $0.86$ times dense at $512$
agents and four retained peers, reproducing the first sweep's $0.85$. Through
FlexAttention the same window costs $1.6$ times dense at $512$ agents: faster
than composing it from library operators, slower than the fused kernel, since
at this size the band still touches several of FlexAttention's $128$-wide
blocks per row.

The end-to-end crossing is a best-case result. It holds on minima in both
sweeps, and under the ratio of medians in the second sweep ($0.92$) but not in the first, where it is $2.10$, because a multi-launch path
has a heavier tail on a contended device than a single dense kernel
(Appendix~\ref{app:prescore}). It appears only at the largest agent count we
measure; at $48$ agents the Z-order path is $16$ times slower than dense. The
section therefore identifies conditions --- a selector cheap in absolute terms,
not only asymptotically, and a fused attention stage behind it --- rather than
recommending this implementation.
