The experiments take up the four quantities of Section~\ref{sec:intro}. We
first check what the composed implementations execute
(Section~\ref{sec:composed}) and whether the executed reduction becomes time
(Section~\ref{sec:latency}). We then ask when a fused score-then-select kernel
is faster than dense attention and use a fixed-set comparison to locate its
time (Section~\ref{sec:fused}), repeat that comparison in reduced precision
(Section~\ref{sec:precision}), and test selectors that act before scoring
(Section~\ref{sec:prescore}). Section~\ref{sec:smalln} covers the agent counts
at which current methods are evaluated, and Section~\ref{sec:checklist} lists
the measurement errors the protocol is designed to prevent.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We time the attention core (from queries, keys and values to the message
tensor), because the input and output projections are shared by every
implementation and dilute the quantity in question. Unless stated otherwise the
configuration is fp32, head width $d{=}16$, batch $256$, on one NVIDIA
Blackwell GPU (sm\_120) under PyTorch 2.11, CUDA 12.8 and Triton 3.6. The dense
baseline is the faster of PyTorch's fused scaled-dot-product path and a
streaming dense kernel we wrote in Triton and tuned over the same parameter
space and admissibility filter as our sparse kernels; the precision study of
Section~\ref{sec:precision} adds FlexAttention \citep{dong2025flexattention}.
Ratios are against that best dense implementation unless a different baseline
is named, and a ratio below one means the sparse path is faster.

Timing uses on-device CUDA events with explicit synchronisation. The device was
shared with other tenants and clocks could not be locked, so implementations
are timed \emph{interleaved}, repetition by repetition, within one sweep per
agent count, and interference lands on all of them together. The primary
statistic is each implementation's minimum over samples, an estimate of its
uncontended cost, and comparisons are ratios of minima. The whole block is
repeated three times. Where a table reports medians, it says whether they are
ratios of per-implementation medians or medians of per-repetition paired ratios.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to the statistic.} We recomputed every comparison at
$192$ agents and above from the medians of the same samples. This changes which
implementation is faster at one of $24$ configurations in the main sweep, and
reverses the end-to-end result for selecting before scoring (the \emph{pre-score}
path) of Section~\ref{sec:prescore} in one of
its two sweeps. The first case is the fused kernel at one retained peer, $384$
agents and $d{=}16$, where one of the three runs carried heavy contention.
Re-timed under CUDA-graph replay in three further sessions, it is faster than
dense on minima and medians alike ($0.92$ on both, every one of nine blocks
between $0.92$ and $0.93$; Table~\ref{tab:crossover}). The second case is
reported with the pre-score results.

\paragraph{Range, verification and other GPUs.} Below $192$ agents a fixed
per-kernel cost is a large share of every measurement, so
Sections~\ref{sec:composed}--\ref{sec:prescore} are read from $192$ agents
upward, and the smaller counts are measured separately under CUDA-graph replay
in Section~\ref{sec:smalln}. Every implementation is checked against a reference
at every configuration whose latency is reported, and all agree to fp32
rounding except at exact ties at the $k$-th score
(Appendix~\ref{app:verification}). Appendix~\ref{sec:protocol} gives the full
protocol, including the tiling search. Appendix~\ref{app:portability} repeats
the headline measurements on an A100, an H100 and an H200, each allocated
exclusively and with every tiling searched again on the device, and reports
where they differ from the Blackwell card.

\subsection{What the composed implementations execute}
\label{sec:composed}

Whether the reduction of Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} is executed depends on
the implementation, and an operation count does not say which. PyTorch's FLOP
counter, attached to the running program, confirms that the gathered
implementation executes the reduction the proposition permits. The masked
implementation multiplies the discarded entries as zeros and so executes dense
arithmetic plus selection, while a count based on the retained fraction credits
it with a saving of about $75\%$ at $N{=}512$, $k{=}127$, where about a quarter
of the peers are retained.

Executing less arithmetic does not make the composed path faster. Built from
general-purpose operators, the gathered path is slower than every dense
implementation at every agent count we measure, by up to a factor of
thirty-seven at $512$ agents and about a quarter of peers retained
(Table~\ref{tab:headline}).

The byte criterion of Section~\ref{sec:roofline} is consistent with this. At
$d{=}16$ and $N{=}512$ the quarter-of-peers operating point is four times past
its leading-order bound, and by the analytic count the sparse contraction moves
about four times the bytes of the dense multiply it replaces. Timed in
isolation, the two run near the bandwidth the device attains on a large copy, so
neither is on a worse hardware path. The criterion does not account for the
largest stage, however. Selection runs at under a tenth of attainable bandwidth
(Appendix~\ref{app:stages}), and on the H100 and H200 hardware counters
attribute most of the composed path's traffic to forming and ranking the scores
rather than to compaction (Appendix~\ref{app:portability}).

These are attention-stage measurements, and the gaps narrow over a wider scope.
The ordering survives the backward pass with a smaller margin: at $512$ agents
the gathered path is $18.4$ times the fused dense path on the forward pass and
$6.2$ times on forward and backward, since selection has almost no adjoint (the
baseline here is the library's fused kernel, which autograd can differentiate;
against our tuned kernel the forward ratio is the thirty-seven above). In a
complete MAPPO \citep{yu2022mappo} iteration at six to twenty-four agents, the environment and
per-step action selection take $78$ to $96\%$ of the time, and all
implementations are within $2\%$ of one another (Appendix~\ref{app:backward}).
In that pipeline neither the attention stage's speedups nor its slowdowns are
visible end to end.

\label{sec:fused}

Most of the composed path's time goes to moving intermediates that a fused
kernel need not create. We wrote fused kernels that hold each query's score row
on chip and never write a score matrix, an index tensor or a gathered value
tensor to memory. At $512$ agents retaining a single peer, the composed sparse
path costs $4.62$\,ms and the fused one $0.139$\,ms. That factor of $33$ comes
from the implementation, since the algorithm is unchanged. The fused path is then faster
than the best dense implementation (Figure~\ref{fig:ksweep}). The $k{=}1$ case
is degenerate as attention, since a softmax over one element is identically one
and the mechanism reduces to copying the highest-scoring peer's value; the
streaming kernel below extends the fp32 advantage to two retained peers at
head widths $16$ and $32$.

Matched authorship is a weaker condition than matched efficiency, so we searched
both kernels over one parameter space (query tile, key tile or head chunk, warp
count and pipeline depth) under one shared-memory admissibility filter, and
report the fraction of the fp32 matrix-multiply rate, measured in the same
process, that each reaches (Table~\ref{tab:autotune},
Appendix~\ref{app:selection}). At head widths $16$ and $32$ the fused sparse
kernel is the \emph{less} efficient of the two at every agent count, reaching
$21$ to $33\%$ of the device's rate against $30$ to $47\%$ for dense, and it is
faster at one retained peer only because it performs about half the arithmetic.
The search is not equal. The sparse kernel must hold an $N$-wide score tile, so
above $192$ agents the filter admits three sparse configurations against $28$ to
$106$ dense ones. The sparse efficiency figures are therefore lower bounds, the
negative entries at head width $64$ and above are weakly supported, and the
one-peer speedup is conservative. The sparse kernel is the best
implementation we found under these constraints; we do not claim it is optimal.

For kernels that keep the score row resident, the advantage is narrow. It
appears at one retained peer, and at two only at $d{=}32$, $N{=}512$. At four
retained peers the fused kernel is $1.3$ to $2.3$ times slower than dense, and
at about a quarter of peers more than an order of magnitude slower. We tried three
ways of selecting inside the kernel: repeated argmax, bisection on the score's
bit pattern, and one sort of score--index pairs. The first costs time linear in
$k$, the second pays for a dense aggregation, and the third pays for a sort that
costs five to sixteen times dense attention; none changes the outcome
(Appendix~\ref{app:selection}).

\paragraph{Where the fused kernel's time goes.} We held the retained set fixed
and changed only how it is found (Table~\ref{tab:fixedset}). Given the exact
top-$k$ indices for free, a fused kernel that reads one key and one value row
per retained peer costs $0.06$ to $0.11$ times dense attention at $512$ agents,
$d{=}16$ and $k \le 4$; the fused score-then-select kernel, attending over the
same peers, costs $0.77$ to $1.93$ times. From $384$ agents upward, at least
$90\%$ of the fused kernel's device time is the cost of finding the set: forming
every score, which Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} shows cannot be removed, and
ranking them, which the proposition does not count. The comparison does not
separate those two parts, but only the ranking grows with $k$. Finding the same
set with library operators instead, a score product followed by
\texttt{torch.topk}, costs $8$ to $27$ times dense attention on its own at
$d{=}16$. This sweep ran on the host's second Blackwell card, and
the fused kernel's ratios reproduce it ($0.77$, $1.27$ and $1.93$ against
$0.81$, $1.27$ and $1.96$ at $d{=}16$, $N{=}512$, $k \in \{1,2,4\}$).

Exact score-then-select requires all $N$ scores to be \emph{computed}, but not
to be held on chip at once. Keeping them \emph{resident} is a design choice of
the kernels above, and it is this choice that restricts their tiling.

\paragraph{Streaming the selection.} To remove that restriction we wrote a
streaming exact top-$k$ kernel in the manner of the chunked selection of
\citet{gupta2021topk}. Each program takes a tile of queries and walks the keys
in tiles, as a dense streaming kernel does, merging each score tile into a
running set of $k$ candidates held in registers. Each score is packed with its
index so that one integer comparison orders both, as \texttt{torch.topk} does on
ties. Nothing $N$-wide stays on chip, so the kernel admits the dense kernel's
tilings and has no cap on $N$.

On the Blackwell card the streaming kernel is faster than the best dense
implementation at every configuration measured with one or two retained peers
(Table~\ref{tab:streaming}), at \StreamKOneLo{} to \StreamKTwoHi{} times its
latency up to $512$ agents. With four it is within about \StreamKFourDev{}\% of
parity (\StreamKFourLo{} to \StreamKFourHi{} up to $512$ agents), where the
resident kernel is $1.3$ to $2.3$ times slower, and with eight it is slower
again up to $512$ agents. With two retained peers the softmax is no longer
trivial, so the fp32 advantage of score-then-select over dense attention is not
confined to the degenerate case. The advantage is still bounded: the score
product, about half of dense attention's operations, sets a floor on the
operation count, and from $384$ agents upward
\StreamFindLo{} to \StreamFindHi{}\% of the streaming kernel's time still goes
to finding the set. At $1024$ agents and $d{=}32$ the one-peer ratio is $0.46$
(Table~\ref{tab:streaming}), a $2.17\times$ wall-clock speedup. The $<2\times$
ceiling in Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} applies to multiply--accumulate counts,
not wall-clock speedup; runtime ratios can cross that value when dense and
sparse kernels execute their operations at different hardware efficiencies.

Beyond one retained peer the window depends on the GPU
(Appendix~\ref{app:portability}). On the A100, H100 and H200 the streaming
kernel is faster than dense at one retained peer at every configuration
(\PortStreamOneLo{} to \PortStreamOneHi{}). With two it is faster at every
configuration on the A100 (\PortStreamTwoAHundredLo{} to
\PortStreamTwoAHundredHi{}) and at \PortStreamTwoHopperWins{} of
\PortStreamTwoConfigs{} configurations on each of the H100 and H200, slower on both at
$d{=}\PortStreamTwoHopperWorstD{}$, $N{=}\PortStreamTwoHopperWorstN{}$
(\PortStreamTwoHopperWorst{}). With four it is slower at every configuration
(\PortStreamFourLo{} to \PortStreamFourHi{}). As on the Blackwell card, finding
the set takes \PortStreamFindLo{} to \PortStreamFindHi{}\% of its time from
$384$ agents upward. These statements hold at the batch of $256$ used
throughout; at a batch of $32$ the one-peer result is less uniform
(Table~\ref{tab:portbatch}).

\subsection{Numerical precision}
\label{sec:precision}

The fused advantage above is measured in fp32 with IEEE products, which keeps
every kernel off the tensor cores so that it reproduces the reference summation
order. In this format dense attention gains the least from the hardware. We
repeated the comparison in TF32 and bf16, searching both kernels again in each
format under the same admissibility filter, and added FlexAttention
\citep{dong2025flexattention} twice: as a dense baseline, and as PyTorch's
route to data-dependent sparsity, with the top-$k$ retained set expressed as a
block mask (Table~\ref{tab:precision}).

Tensor cores help dense attention far more than they help the resident fused
top-$k$ kernel. The best dense implementation becomes $2.7$ to $4.0$ times faster in
TF32 than in fp32, and $3.9$ to $8.1$ times faster in bf16. The resident kernel at
one retained peer, faster than dense at every agent count in fp32 ($0.67$ to
$0.91$), is slower from $384$ agents upward in both reduced formats ($1.07$ to
$1.38$), and at $192$ agents ($0.83$ to $1.01$) it ranges from faster to parity.
One reduced-precision cell is at parity rather than slower under the ratio of
medians ($0.99$, bf16, $d{=}16$, $N{=}512$).

In fp32, dense efficiency and head width move together, so the fp32 data alone
could not say whether the resident kernel's crossover is confined to narrow
heads because of the head width or because dense attention runs far from the
machine at those widths (Appendix~\ref{app:selection}). At a fixed head width,
raising the efficiency of dense attention removes the resident kernel's
crossover from $384$ agents upward, which favours
the second reading. The test is not perfectly controlled, since reduced
precision also accelerates the sparse kernel's score product.

TF32 also changes the function computed. Rounding the products reorders
near-tied scores, so every top-$k$ implementation (fused, composed or
FlexAttention) retains a different peer from a float64 reference in up to
$0.7\%$ of query rows, rising with $k$. We exclude configurations above $0.1\%$,
which removes most TF32 entries beyond one retained peer; since cost rises with
$k$, those entries could only be slower than the one-peer entries that remain.

FlexAttention is the fastest dense implementation in nine of the twelve
reduced-precision configurations, which supports the choice of baseline. As a
route to top-$k$ sparsity it is $8$ to $218$ times slower than dense attention.
The retained set must first be ranked from a full score matrix and converted
into a block mask on every call, and at $128$-wide blocks every block of the
mask contains a retained pair, so the kernel then computes densely.

\paragraph{The streaming kernel in reduced precision.} On the Blackwell card the
streaming kernel (Table~\ref{tab:streamprec} in Appendix~\ref{app:streamprec})
stays faster than dense at one retained peer in TF32 (\SPTfOneLo{} to
\SPTfOneHi{}) and is near parity in bf16 (\SPBfOneLo{} to \SPBfOneHi{}), where
the resident kernel was slower from $384$ agents up. With two retained peers it
is faster or at parity in the TF32 configurations that pass the retained-set
filter (\SPTfTwoLo{} to \SPTfTwoHi{}) and slower in bf16 (\SPBfTwoLo{} to
\SPBfTwoHi{}); with four it is \SPBfFourLo{} to \SPBfFourHi{} times slower in
bf16, and no TF32 configuration passes. Reduced precision accelerates every
matrix product, the score product included, but not the integer comparisons of
selection, so the narrower the format, the larger the share of the sparse
kernel's time that selection takes.

\paragraph{bf16 on four GPUs.} Attention commonly runs in bf16 in
mixed-precision training and inference, and there the fp32 result does not carry
over. On the A100, H100 and H200 the streaming kernel is slower than the best
bf16 dense implementation at every configuration and retained count we
measured: \PortBfOneLo{} to \PortBfOneHi{} times dense at one retained peer,
\PortBfTwoLo{} to \PortBfTwoHi{} at two and \PortBfFourLo{} to \PortBfFourHi{}
at four. On the Blackwell card it is near parity at one (\SPBfOneLo{} to
\SPBfOneHi{}) and slower beyond. The retained set is not the cause: bf16 changed
the retained peers in at most \PortBfMismatchMax{} of the \PortBfRowsM{} million
query rows checked on any of the four GPUs, so the difference from fp32 is one
of cost. TF32 sits between the two, at \PortTfOneLo{} to \PortTfOneHi{} at one
retained peer off the Blackwell card (Appendix~\ref{app:portability}). We timed
the forward pass of the attention core in each format, not a bf16 backward pass
or a training run, so conclusions about mixed-precision training are limited to
that scope. Within it, a score-then-select kernel of this design gives no
latency gain over dense attention in bf16 on three of the four GPUs.

\subsection{Selecting before scoring}
\label{sec:prescore}

We test the escape case of SCALE, Proposition~\ref{prop:escape}, with three
selectors that span cases (b) to (d) of Corollary~\ref{cor:taxonomy}; the first
two follow neighbourhood rules used by multi-agent mechanisms and the third
comes from sequence models. \emph{All-pairs nearest neighbours} ranks the
distances between two-dimensional positions, as a neighbourhood rule recomputed
each step does \citep{jiang2018dgn,jiang2018atoc}. It is case~(b), and its
count ratio rises towards $d$ and no further. \emph{Grid nearest neighbours}
buckets agents in a uniform grid and ranks only the candidates in the
surrounding $3 \times 3$ cells. Its cost is $\Theta(Nk)$ plus one sort of the
agents, so it is formally case~(c) because of the comparison sort and
effectively case~(d), since the sort is under $5\%$ of its count here; it
retains the exact nearest neighbours in at least $99.93\%$ of cases for
uniformly placed agents. \emph{Z-order} orders agents along a Morton curve and
keeps the $k$ adjacent in that order, as ZETA does for low-dimensional key
projections in sequence models \citep{zeng2025zeta}; its sort is
$O(N \log N)$, case~(c). Closed forms are in Appendix~\ref{app:closedforms}.
Attention then runs over the retained pairs only, composed from library
operators or as the fused fixed-set kernel of Section~\ref{sec:fused}; the
Z-order window, a band in curve order, also runs through FlexAttention. These
selectors attend to different peers than top-$k$, so this comparison is of
cost, not of quality; Table~\ref{tab:fixedset} is the comparison at a fixed set.

\paragraph{The attention stage.} Once the retained set is known, the fused
attention over it behaves as the accounting predicts. At two retained peers it
costs $0.06$ times dense attention at $512$ agents on the Blackwell card, more
than sixteen times faster, and is \PortPreAttnLo{} to \PortPreAttnHi{} times
faster on the A100, H100 and H200. Its advantage grows with the agent count,
because the score term now shrinks with the aggregation
(Figure~\ref{fig:prescore} in Appendix~\ref{app:prescore}). Composed from
library operators the same stage is slower than dense attention at every agent
count and sparsity measured, and fusing it is worth a factor of $4$ to $17$
(Appendix~\ref{app:prescore}). No score-then-select implementation approaches
these ratios, because none can remove the score term.

The Blackwell figure depends on the estimator and on contention. The ratio of medians is $0.064$ in that sweep but $0.18$ in the second, more
contended one. Under CUDA-graph replay the same kernel over the exact top-$k$ set costs $0.07$
(Table~\ref{tab:fixedset}), so the figure holds as a device time even though
the eager ratio depends on contention. The kernel
runs for about ten microseconds, and an eager deployment whose host launch path
is slower than that will not see the full gain.

\paragraph{End to end.} The end-to-end comparison is at four retained peers
(Table~\ref{tab:selectors}) and was run in two interleaved sweeps; the second
adds the grid selector and FlexAttention. The all-pairs selector, whose class is capped at a
constant, is the most expensive at $512$ agents, by more than its operation
count implies; the order of the other two is set by constants rather than by
class, and at $192$ agents the grid path is slower than the all-pairs one. The all-pairs selector's cost grows quadratically, from $0.32$ to
$5.69$\,ms between $192$ and $512$ agents, and its path ends $37$ times slower
than dense attention in this sweep. The cap therefore binds far below its
analytic value, consistent with a selector whose cost is traffic rather than
arithmetic. The grid selector removes the quadratic growth, and its cost rises
from $0.51$ to $1.67$\,ms over the same range. Gathering and ranking about a
hundred candidates per agent still keeps the path $10$ times slower than dense
at $512$ agents, and at sixteen retained peers the grid path is slower than the
all-pairs one at $384$ and $512$ agents. Within the measured range, then, the
asymptotic class does not fix the ordering. The Z-order selector's cost is
nearly flat, $0.10$ to $0.14$\,ms, so its share falls as attention grows more
expensive: the path costs $0.86$ times dense at $512$ agents and four retained peers, reproducing the first sweep's $0.85$. Through
FlexAttention the same window costs $1.6$ times dense at $512$ agents, faster
than composing it from library operators and slower than the fused kernel,
since at this size the band still touches several of FlexAttention's
$128$-wide blocks per row.

The end-to-end crossing holds only in the best case. It holds on minima in both
sweeps, and under the ratio of medians in the second sweep ($0.92$) but not in the
first, where it is $2.10$, because a multi-launch path has a heavier tail on a
contended device than a single dense kernel (Appendix~\ref{app:prescore}). It
also appears only at the largest agent count we measure; at $48$ agents the
Z-order path is $16$ times slower than dense. And it is specific to the
Blackwell card: on the A100, H100 and H200 the fused attention stage still costs
\PortZorderAttendLo{} to \PortZorderAttendHi{} times dense, but the whole
Z-order path costs \PortZorderTotalLo{} to \PortZorderTotalHi{}
(Appendix~\ref{app:portability}). Selecting before scoring paid only under two
conditions, a selector that is cheap in absolute terms and not only
asymptotically, and a fused attention stage. The Z-order path is evidence for
those conditions; we do not offer it as a design. Of the three predictions of
Section~\ref{sec:roofline}, the ceiling held as a bound on executed operations
(Section~\ref{sec:composed}), although measured time ratios are not bound by it
(Section~\ref{sec:fused}); the
byte screen
was consistent with the composed path's losses but did not separate wins from
losses, and the class ordering held for the all-pairs selector at $512$ agents but did
not fix the order of the others.

At these sizes device time follows the number of kernels more closely than the
modelled operation count. Within each configuration of agent count and
batch, the rank correlation between device time and kernel count has a median
of \SmallNRhoLaunch{}, against \SmallNRhoFlops{} for modelled executed
operations. On the host, issuing a call costs about \SmallNPerLaunch{}\,\textmu s
per kernel on top of a fixed \SmallNHostFixed{}\,\textmu s; an eager deployment
pays this host cost as well, and it bounds latency from below whenever it
exceeds the device time. Every composed path, sparse or dense, issues six to
thirteen kernels and is \SmallNComposedLo{} to \SmallNComposedHi{} times slower
than the best dense kernel at every agent count from $12$ to $96$ (across
retained counts). The Z-order path, whose selector issues more than fifty
kernels, is \SmallNZorderLo{} to \SmallNZorderHi{} times slower, so the
pre-score result of Section~\ref{sec:prescore}, observed only at $512$ agents,
does not extend to this range in this implementation. Kernel count does not
decide everything. Among single-kernel paths, the resident fused
score-then-select kernel at one retained peer is at parity at $12$ agents and
$5$ to $15\%$ faster than dense from $24$ to $96$, and at two retained peers it
is within $6\%$ of parity throughout; the fixed-set kernel, which issues two
kernels, is slower than dense at $12$ agents and faster from $48$. The
association with kernel count is not specific to the Blackwell card: on the
A100, H100 and H200 the same median rank correlation is
\PortSmallNRhoLaunchLo{} to \PortSmallNRhoLaunchHi{} for kernel count and at
most \PortSmallNRhoFlopsHi{} for modelled executed operations
(Appendix~\ref{app:portability}). At the agent counts where current methods are
evaluated, how many kernels a communication step issues is more closely
associated with its device time than whether the step is sparse.

\subsection{A checklist for benchmarking small kernels}
\label{sec:checklist}

Attention over tens to hundreds of agents runs for microseconds, on a device
that is often shared, against library kernels tuned for other shapes. Each of
those conditions produced at least one error in this study that yielded a
plausible number, and Table~\ref{tab:pitfalls} lists them with the practice that
prevents each. Two of the eight deserve emphasis, because nothing in the
resulting numbers betrays them. A masked implementation benchmarked as sparse
executes none of the counted saving, and a hand-written sparse kernel timed
against a vendor's dense kernel measures authorship as well as sparsity. The
first is caught by counting the operations the running program issues, the
second by searching both kernels over one parameter space and reporting the
fraction of the machine each reaches. Appendix~\ref{sec:pitfalls} describes each
entry and how it was found.
