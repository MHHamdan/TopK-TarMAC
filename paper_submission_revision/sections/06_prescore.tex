Proposition~\ref{prop:escape} says that a selector fixing the retained set
before any score exists is not bound by the twofold ceiling, and that its
operation-count saving grows without limit as the retained set shrinks. That is
a statement about counts, and this paper is about what counts fail to predict.
This section instantiates the escape and measures it.

We use two selectors, both corresponding to mechanisms multi-agent
communication already uses. The first retains each agent's $k$ nearest peers by
Euclidean distance over two-dimensional positions, which is what
neighbourhood-based communication does \citep{jiang2018dgn,jiang2018atoc}. Its
selector is still pairwise, but pairwise in two dimensions rather than in the
head dimension, so it costs a factor of $d/2$ less than forming the scores. The
second orders agents along a Morton curve and retains the $k$ agents adjacent in
that order, at $O(N \log N)$, which satisfies the $o(Nkd)$ condition of
case~(i). Both then evaluate attention scores for the retained pairs only, so
the score term falls from $N^2d$ to $Nkd$ and the ceiling does not apply.

The two selectors attend to different peers than a top-$k$ rule would, so what
follows compares cost and not quality. Selection and attention are timed
separately as well as together, so that the selector is charged and not assumed
away. Every ratio is against the faster of the library dense kernel and our
own, for the reason Section~\ref{sec:pitfalls} gives.

The operation-count saving is exactly what the proposition predicts. At $512$
agents retaining four peers, the Z-order path performs $104$ times fewer
operations than dense attention and the nearest-neighbour path $18$ times fewer,
the difference between them being the cost of their selectors. Neither is faster
than dense attention at any agent count we measure, and the best either comes is
$1.8$ times dense.

Two separate things go wrong, and only the first is the one we expected.

The selector can cost more than the attention it is choosing for. The
nearest-neighbour selector materialises an $N \times N$ distance matrix and
ranks it, which are the same two operations, on the same shape, that
Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} says a score-then-select rule cannot avoid.
Working in two dimensions rather than in the head dimension buys a factor in the
arithmetic and nothing in the traffic, and traffic is what governs
(Section~\ref{sec:stages}); by $512$ agents its selector alone costs $27$ times
the whole of dense attention. The Z-order selector avoids the quadratic
structure and its cost is flat in the agent count, but flat is not small: it
sits between $0.13$ and $0.37$\,ms across our range, which is comparable to or
larger than the entire dense attention it is displacing at every agent count.
It is also composed from library operators, so like the composed sparse path of
Section~\ref{sec:composed} its constant is an upper bound rather than a property
of the design.

The second failure is the one that matters, because no better selector can
repair it. Set the selector aside entirely and time only the attention over an
already-chosen peer set --- scoring $Nk$ pairs instead of $N^2$, then aggregating
over $k$. That stage is barely cheaper than dense attention anywhere and is
usually dearer. Its best case over the whole grid is $0.85$ times dense, at
$384$ agents retaining two peers, where the operation count promises a factor of
$132$; at four retained peers it never falls below $1.01$, and at the smallest
agent count it costs $1.7$ to $1.9$ times dense. Two orders of magnitude of
arithmetic buy, at the very best, fifteen per cent.

The reason is the one Section~\ref{sec:composed} identifies for the
score-then-select path, and it survives the change of selector because it was
never about selection. Scoring $Nk$ chosen pairs requires gathering $k$ keys and
$k$ values per query, so the operand is $2kd$ elements per query where dense
attention reads a contiguous $N$-row block and multiplies it as a matrix
product. At $512$ agents, four retained peers and head width $16$ that is $128$
gathered elements per query against a $512$-wide contiguous row, and the gathered
form loses the matrix-multiply shape as well. The arithmetic falls by two orders
of magnitude and the traffic does not. We did not fuse this stage, and by the
standard Section~
ef{sec:fused} sets that makes its constant an upper bound
rather than a property of the design: a fused pre-score attention kernel is the
obvious next measurement, and until it exists the explanation above is an
inference from the accounting and from the score-then-select case rather than an
independently controlled result.

What this leaves of Proposition~\ref{prop:escape} is worth being exact about,
because the proposition is not wrong. Removing the full score matrix genuinely
removes the ceiling: the count ratios in Table~\ref{tab:prescore} are far above
the factor of two that bounds any score-then-select rule, and no implementation
elsewhere in this paper achieves anything like them. The proposition simply
turns out to bound the wrong quantity twice over. Its selector term is charged
in operations, and the nearest-neighbour selector shows that a selector can be
cheap in operations and ruinous in traffic. And the term it leaves --- attention
over $Nk$ pairs --- is itself priced in operations, and at these sizes that
price is not what the hardware charges.

A successor design is therefore accountable for three things rather than one,
and an operation count shows only the first: what the selector costs to run,
whether it reaches its decision without materialising a structure of the size it
meant to avoid, and whether the sparse attention it leaves behind can be
executed in a shape the hardware is fast at. The third is the one this section
adds, and it is the reason block-level selection works in long-context attention
while per-pair selection does not work here. A block of a long sequence is still
a dense matrix multiply after selection; a handful of gathered peers is not.
