Proposition~\ref{prop:escape} says that a selector which fixes the retained set
before any score exists is not bound by the twofold ceiling, and that its
operation-count saving grows without limit as the retained set shrinks. That is
a statement about counts, and the rest of this paper is about what counts fail
to predict. This section instantiates the escape and measures it.

Two selectors, both chosen because multi-agent communication already uses
mechanisms of their kind rather than because they are new. The first retains
each agent's $k$ nearest peers by Euclidean distance in the two-dimensional
position space, which is what neighbourhood-based communication does
\citep{jiang2018dgn,jiang2018atoc}; its selector is still pairwise, but pairwise
in two dimensions rather than in the head dimension, so it costs a factor of
$d/2$ less than forming the scores. The second orders agents along a Morton
curve and retains the $k$ agents adjacent in that order, which costs
$O(N \log N)$ and so satisfies the $o(Nkd)$ condition of case~(i) of the
proposition for any fixed $k$. Both then evaluate attention scores for the
retained pairs only, so the score term falls from $N^2d$ to $Nkd$ and the
ceiling does not apply.

The two selectors attend to different peers than a top-$k$ rule would, so what
follows is a comparison of cost and not of quality. Selection and attention are
timed separately as well as together, because a proposition that charges for the
selector is only tested by an experiment that charges for it.

Both selectors deliver what the accounting promises and neither delivers a
speedup, and the reasons differ in a way that identifies a second condition the
count cannot express.

The nearest-neighbour selector removes an order of magnitude of arithmetic and
gets slower, relative to dense attention, as the agent count rises: at $512$
agents it performs roughly twenty times fewer operations than dense attention
and takes fifteen times longer. Its selector is quadratic, so it materialises an
$N \times N$ distance matrix and ranks it --- the same two operations, on the
same shape, that Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} says a score-then-select rule
cannot avoid. Working in two dimensions rather than in the head dimension buys a
factor in the arithmetic and nothing in the traffic, and Section~\ref{sec:stages}
shows traffic to be what governs. A pre-score selector of this kind escapes the
proposition on paper and reproduces its cost structure in silicon.

The Morton selector behaves in the opposite direction and comes close. Its cost
is essentially independent of the agent count over our whole range, because a
sort of a few hundred keys per batch element is bound by kernel launch rather
than by work, and its attention stage --- scoring and aggregating only the
retained pairs --- is roughly twice as fast as dense attention at the largest
agent count, which is what Proposition~\ref{prop:escape} predicts once the score
term shrinks with the aggregation. The ratio against dense attention therefore
improves monotonically with the agent count, from an order of magnitude at $48$
agents to within a small margin of parity at $512$. What keeps it above parity
is not the attention at all but the fixed cost of the sort, which at these sizes
is comparable to the entire dense attention it is trying to displace.

Three conclusions follow, and the third is the one we would have missed without
this measurement.

Escaping the operation-count ceiling is necessary. The Morton selector's
attention stage is genuinely faster than dense attention, and no score-then-select
implementation in this paper achieves that at any comparable sparsity, because
none of them can remove the score term.

Escaping it is not sufficient, and the accounting says nothing about which of
the two selectors will be faster. Both satisfy the proposition; one improves with
scale and one deteriorates. What separates them is whether the selector reaches
its decision through a structure of the size it was introduced to avoid.
Proposition~\ref{prop:escape} charges for the selector in operations, and
operations are the wrong currency: the nearest-neighbour selector's arithmetic is
small and its traffic is exactly the traffic the ceiling is about.

And a selector must be cheap in absolute terms at the size in question, not
merely asymptotically. The Morton selector is asymptotically free relative to
attention and is nonetheless the reason its path does not win, because a few
hundred agents is small enough that a launch-bound sort costs as much as the
quadratic work it is avoiding. This is the same quantity that makes block-level
selection succeed in long-context attention, where a selector amortised over
blocks of a long sequence is negligible against the work it skips
\citep{yuan2025nsa,lai2026msa}, and it is the quantity a successor method for
multi-agent communication has to get right. Nothing about the analysis suggests
this is impossible; it does suggest that the design target is the selector's
constant, not the aggregation's asymptotics.
