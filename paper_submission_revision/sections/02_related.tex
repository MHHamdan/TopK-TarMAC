\subsection{Communication sparsity in multi-agent reinforcement learning}

Learned inter-agent communication has converged on attention as the mechanism
for deciding whose message matters. CommNet \citep{sukhbaatar2016commnet}
broadcasts mean-pooled hidden states; TarMAC \citep{das2019tarmac} introduces
signature-based targeting so that receivers weight senders; MAT
\citep{wen2022mat} casts the joint policy as a sequence model. Applied systems
inherit the pattern, pairing heterogeneous learners with attention-based message
aggregators \citep{hamdan2026marliotp}. Since attention over $N$ agents is
pairwise, a large part of this literature also restricts who talks to whom, and
the restriction is usually motivated by cost. DGN observes that receiving a
large amount of information ``requires high bandwidth and incurs high
computational complexity'' \citep{jiang2018dgn}, a claim about transmitted bytes
and about local compute, of which only the second is what we measure.

Proposition~\ref{prop:escape} organises these restriction mechanisms by what
their selector costs, and the resulting three groups behave differently. The
first fixes the retained set from structure that is available before any score
exists: DGN restricts attention to each agent's nearest neighbours in the
environment \citep{jiang2018dgn}, ATOC's initiator forms a group from its
nearest collaborators within a communication field \citep{jiang2018atoc}, and
mean-field methods replace the peer set with a single averaged interaction
\citep{yang2018meanfield}. What such a selector costs depends on something the
operation count of the attention itself does not record. If the neighbour list
arrives with the environment, or is maintained incrementally, or is a fixed
topology, the selector is free and the mechanism lands in
Corollary~\ref{cor:taxonomy}(d). If instead it is recomputed each step from all
pairwise positions --- which is how a neighbourhood rule is ordinarily
implemented when the agents move --- then it is an all-pairs selector in a
low-dimensional feature space, case~(b), and its saving is capped by a constant
however sparse the attention becomes. Section~\ref{sec:prescore} measures the
second version and finds the cap binding. The second group scores something cheaper
than the full pairwise interaction: IC3Net gates whether an agent speaks at all
\citep{singh2018ic3net}, and SchedNet ranks agents by a scalar importance weight
and schedules the $k$ largest \citep{kim2019schednet}. These are top-$k$ rules,
but over an $O(N)$ vector rather than an $N \times N$ matrix, so
Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} does not bind them. The third group runs a
pairwise selector before running pairwise attention: G2ANet decides which of all
$N^2$ edges survive with a hard-attention network and then applies soft
attention to the survivors \citep{liu2020g2anet}, and MAGIC builds a hard or
soft adjacency from an all-pairs interaction before its graph attention
\citep{niu2021magic}. Their arithmetic saving is real in the aggregation and
paid for in the selector, at the same order as the score matrix they avoid.

The mechanism this paper analyses --- selecting peers by the attention score
itself, after computing all of them --- is the limiting case of that third
group, and it is rare in multi-agent communication, not absent. The
closest published relative is the adaptive sparse communication graph of
\citet{sun2020sparsecomm}, which replaces softmax with a generalisation of
sparsemax so that the normalisation itself emits exact zeros. It motivates the
design partly through scalability, stating that the resulting sparsity ``can
reduce the complexity of both the forward pass and the back-propagation of the
policy and value networks'', and it explicitly considers and rejects hard
top-$k$ thresholding as non-differentiable. Because the sparsity-inducing map is
applied to logits already formed for every pair, its selection is post-score and
sits inside the class Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} constrains. That places
the method in our accounting and does not
contradict its claims: its reported gains are in sample efficiency at up
to $30$ agents and it reports no measured runtime. Handshake-style mechanisms
that match a requester's query against every candidate key and then select
\citep{liu2020who2com} are score-then-select in the same sense.

The top-$k$ design analysed here is the direct construction from the problem
setting of Section~\ref{sec:setting}; the ceiling constrains it, and the
structural selectors above are outside it. And the agent counts at which these methods operate are small:
between two and roughly thirty agents for the attention-based communication
methods above, with DGN reaching the low hundreds in grid worlds. Our sweep runs
to $N{=}512$ so that the trend is visible, but the regime the argument is about
is $N \lesssim 10^2$, and beyond it the field has already abandoned all-pairs
interaction in favour of neighbourhoods and mean fields --- which is to say, in
favour of selectors fixed before any score exists.

\subsection{Sparse and efficient attention}

The efficient-attention literature is where the operation-count argument for
sparsity is most developed, and its mechanisms separate along the same axis.
Longformer \citep{beltagy2020longformer} and BigBird \citep{zaheer2020bigbird}
fix which pairs interact in advance through sliding windows, global tokens and
random edges, so the score computation itself becomes sparse and the saving is
asymptotic; their selector is essentially free. Reformer
\citep{kitaev2020reformer} restricts candidate interactions by a
locality-sensitive hash and Routing Transformer \citep{roy2021routing} by online
$k$-means clustering; both are content-dependent, both evaluate scores only for
candidate pairs, and together they are why the escape proposition carries a
selector term at all. Performer \citep{choromanski2021performers} falls outside
the premise entirely: it keeps every interaction and reorders the algebra
through a kernel factorisation.

Top-$k$ attention in the sequence setting is the closest external analogue of
the mechanism we build, and the comparison turns on which efficiency is being
claimed. \citet{gupta2021topk} process queries in chunks and, for each query,
compute its $k$ largest scores against all keys exactly --- a score-then-select
rule in our sense --- and their stated benefit is memory: chunking reduces peak
attention memory from $\Omega(L_Q L_K)$ to $O(C L_K)$ for chunk size $C$ while
remaining a drop-in replacement in pretrained models. Memory efficiency,
operation-count efficiency and wall-clock efficiency are distinct quantities,
and a mechanism can improve one at the expense of another; ours is a question
about the second and the third.

A related body of work asks how much of the attention function survives
truncation, bounding the approximation error of keeping only the largest
entries. That question is orthogonal to ours. We hold the function fixed --- our
gathered, masked and fused top-$k$ implementations agree to floating-point
tolerance --- and ask whether the sparsification reduces executed computation
and elapsed time. A method could be exact and slow, or approximate and fast, and
the two literatures answer different halves of the design question.

Sparsemax \citep{martins2016sparsemax} and $\alpha$-entmax
\citep{peters2019entmax} obtain exact zeros from the normalisation rather than a
hard cutoff, and make no claim about attention cost; that their zeros arrive
after the scores exist, and are therefore subject to our ceiling, is an
inference we draw, not a claim they make. Gumbel-softmax
\citep{jang2017gumbel} supplies the relaxation used by learned discrete gates,
and constrained-RL formulations \citep{achiam2017cpo,stooke2020pidlagrangian}
the machinery for optimising under a communication budget. We implement entmax
and a Lagrangian budget as variants in Section~\ref{sec:training}.

Score-then-select is used at scale in sparse mixture-of-experts, where a router
scores every expert and dispatches each token to the best $k$
\citep{shazeer2017moe,fedus2022switch}. The structure of the argument is the one
we examine, and so is the exemption: these architectures are designed so that
the router is small relative to the expert computation it gates, so the analogue
of our $N^2 d$ score term is a minor cost, not an equal one, and the
saving is real. Whether score-then-select pays therefore depends on the ratio
between the cost of scoring all candidates and the cost of the work being
skipped --- large in mixture-of-experts, and equal to one in single-head
attention, which is the ceiling above.

\subsection{Hardware-aware sparse execution}

That operation counts, memory traffic, kernel fusion and realised latency are
distinct quantities is established in the systems literature. The roofline model
\citep{williams2009roofline} formalises when arithmetic ceases to govern
runtime. FlashAttention \citep{dao2022flashattention} and memory-efficient
attention \citep{rabe2021selfattention} make the point from the other direction:
the winning implementations of dense attention reduce memory traffic without
changing the operation count at all, which is precisely the axis a FLOPs-based
argument cannot see. Longformer anticipates the distinction this paper turns on,
reporting runtime and memory for three implementations of one attention pattern
--- a loop version, a vectorised chunked version and a custom CUDA kernel ---
and
noting that the chunked variant consumes roughly twice the optimal memory
because it computes some zero values.

Recent systems work establishes where sparse attention does pay, and it bounds
our conclusion from the other side. Native Sparse Attention \citep{yuan2025nsa}
ranks compressed key--value blocks and attends within only the selected blocks,
with kernels written for the resulting access pattern, reporting large forward,
backward and decoding speedups at $64$k context; MiniMax Sparse Attention
\citep{lai2026msa} co-designs blockwise selection with its GPU execution path
and reports comparable prefill and decoding gains at $1$M context. Three
properties separate that regime from ours, and all three are load-bearing.
Selection is amortised over blocks rather than performed per pair, so the
selector cost falls by the block size. Block structure preserves the dense
matrix-multiply shape inside each selected tile, so the arithmetic that remains
still runs on the hardware path built for it --- which per-pair gather, whose
aggregation degenerates into a matrix--vector product, does not. And at
$10^5$--$10^6$ tokens the skipped computation is enormous, where at
$N \lesssim 10^2$ it is bounded by Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} at a factor of
two. We take these results at face value and make no claim of comparability
across four orders of magnitude in sequence length; what they identify is what a
successor design in our regime would have to change.

\paragraph{Measurement practice.} Our training protocol follows the
recommendations of \citet{papoudakis2021epymarl} for systematic MARL
benchmarking, and we report effect sizes and intervals in place of bare means
\citep{agarwal2021rliable}. Environments come from the PettingZoo/MPE family
\citep{terry2020pettingzoo} and the learner is MAPPO \citep{yu2022mappo}.
Motivated by Longformer's precedent, we also checked whether the efficiency
claims in the papers we drew on are accompanied by a measurement: among the
multi-agent papers making such a claim, one of five reports a measured temporal
quantity attributable to its mechanism, against five of six among the
efficient-attention and mixture-of-experts papers. Twelve papers support no
conclusion about either field, and we report the asymmetry only because it
characterises the evidence base from which we ourselves adopted the
operation-count argument. Appendix~\ref{app:audit} gives the protocol, the
completed sheet and its scope caveats.
