Sparsification would still be worth having if it cost nothing in return, and
testing that requires a channel that is actually used: on a task where it is not
needed, ``sparsification is free'' and ``the channel was never used'' make
identical predictions. The role of this study is therefore narrow: to
establish that the architecture being sparsified does something. No claim
elsewhere in this paper rests on it.

We train with MAPPO \citep{yu2022mappo} on PettingZoo/MPE
\citep{terry2020pettingzoo}, five seeds per variant and two million environment
steps, following the protocols of \citet{papoudakis2021epymarl} and
\citet{agarwal2021rliable}. Two positive controls test whether the channel
carries something: a reference task in which each agent observes only its
partner's goal, fixed at two agents where selection is degenerate, and a
partially observable variant of a standard cooperative task, introduced here, in
which a peer's relative position is withheld beyond a fixed radius
(Appendix~\ref{app:env}).

Both controls separate completely, each attaining the floor of an exact
permutation test at five seeds (Table~\ref{tab:training}), and the measured
attention entropy on the partially observable control is $0.35$ nats against a
ceiling of $1.61$, so the learned weighting is peaked rather than uniform.
Sparsification is therefore applied to an architecture whose communication
machinery improves return under the evaluated setup. It does not follow that the
attention channel alone accounts for the difference: the communicating variants
carry $7$--$19\%$ more trainable parameters, and the channel-specific claim
would need a parameter-matched sham variant, which we do not have. Comparisons
between sparsification variants were pre-registered with a power calculation
that showed in advance they could resolve only complete separation at five
seeds; all are Holm-adjusted nulls, and they are reported with the
pre-registration in Appendix~\ref{app:training}.

\paragraph{A selector chosen before scoring, in training.} The Z-order selector
of Section~\ref{sec:prescore} is compared there on cost alone, so we added it to
this study after the fact, as a descriptive and unregistered variant on the
partially observable task at six agents and two retained peers, identical to
the attention top-$k$ variant except for the selector. It reads each agent's
own position from its observation, pooled across agents through the channel,
and no other simulator state. Over five seeds its final return is
$-197.8 \pm 5.8$, against $-193.4 \pm 5.5$ for attention top-$k$,
$-197.5 \pm 7.7$ for uniformly random peers and $-204.4 \pm 3.3$ without
communication. The difference from no communication is $+6.7$, with a $95\%$
interval of $[+1.5, +11.8]$; from random peers $-0.3$ $[-7.7, +7.4]$, and from
attention top-$k$ $-4.3$ $[-10.5, +2.1]$. The cheap selector therefore keeps the
benefit of communicating, but on this evidence no more than random peers do: at
six agents $49.5\%$ of the peers a Morton window selects are among the two true
nearest neighbours, against $40\%$ for a random choice, so curve locality is weak at
this size. Whether a selector cheap enough to pay in time is also informative
enough to matter is a question for larger populations, where this one has more
locality to exploit.
