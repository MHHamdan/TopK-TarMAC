The efficiency case for restricting inter-agent attention to each agent's
highest-scoring peers rests on an operation count, and the count is smaller than
it looks. Because the selection reads the scores, every pairwise interaction has
been evaluated before any is discarded, so only the aggregation is sparsifiable
and no choice of sparsity removes more than half of attention's arithmetic. That
bound is not a property of top-$k$ but of the price of the selector: writing it
as one accounting in which a rule is charged for what it costs to decide places
every restriction mechanism on a single axis, and the mechanisms that obtain
asymptotic savings are exactly those whose selectors are cheap.

Measuring what executes shows the bounded saving to be optimistic twice over.
An implementation that applies the selection as a mask performs the dense
aggregation anyway, so it executes no saving at all while a count credits it
with a large one. An implementation that genuinely compacts the retained peers
executes the saving and is slower regardless, because compaction replaces
multiplication by zeros with the writing and re-reading of a gathered tensor,
and at these sizes time follows bytes. The criterion that emerges is simple
enough to check before writing the code: gathering is byte-neutral only when the
compacted operand is no larger than the score row it avoids re-reading.

Whether any of this is a property of the algorithm or of the tools was the
question our earlier measurements could not answer, and writing the kernels
answered it in both directions. Most of the measured penalty is the composition
of general-purpose operators, and a fused kernel removes it. What remains is the
algorithm, and it is decisive at the sparsity levels this literature uses: the
fastest sparse implementation we could write is faster than the fastest dense
implementation we could write only when one or two peers are retained, by a
margin the analytic ceiling bounds, and is an order of magnitude slower once a
realistic number of peers is kept. Two structural reasons survive every
optimisation we tried. Ranking $N$ candidates to keep $k$ of them costs more, on
the units that perform the ranking, than aggregating over the $k$ saves; and a
kernel can make that ranking cheap in $k$ only by expressing the retained set as
a zero weight, which abandons the sparse arithmetic it was built to obtain.
Exact score-then-select attention, fused, can have sparse arithmetic or cheap
selection, and we could not obtain both.

The constructive implication is not that sparse communication is a bad idea but
that the saving has to be taken before the scores exist rather than after. A
selector that fixes the retained set in advance is not bound by the ceiling, and
its promised saving grows without limit as the retained set shrinks. Measuring
two such selectors shows that removing the bound is necessary and not
sufficient, and shows it twice over. A selector that reaches its decision
through a quadratic intermediate --- ranking a matrix of pairwise distances
rather than of pairwise scores --- delivers an order of magnitude of arithmetic
saving and is an order of magnitude slower, because it reproduces in two
dimensions exactly the traffic the ceiling was about. A selector that never
forms such a structure delivers the saving where it matters: its attention stage
is roughly twice as fast as dense attention at the largest agent count we
measure, which no score-then-select implementation in this paper achieves at any
sparsity. Even that one does not win overall, and the reason is the third
finding: its own fixed cost, a sort of a few hundred keys, is comparable at
these sizes to the entire dense attention it is displacing.

A successor method is therefore accountable for three things, of which an
operation count shows only the first: what its selector costs in operations,
whether it reaches its decision without materialising a structure of the size it
means to avoid, and whether its constant is small against the work it skips at
the size in question. The third is why block-level selection succeeds in
long-context attention and why the same idea does not transfer unchanged to a
few hundred agents: a selector amortised over the blocks of a hundred-thousand
token sequence is negligible against what it skips, while the same selector
against a few hundred agents is not. That is a statement about a ratio rather
than about a mechanism, and it is checkable in advance, which is the most useful
thing this analysis produces.
