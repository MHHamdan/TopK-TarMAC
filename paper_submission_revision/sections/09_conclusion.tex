We characterised when sparse attention in multi-agent communication turns an
operation-count saving into a saving in time. For score-then-select rules the
answer is bounded before any measurement: because the selection reads every
score, at most half of the forward-pass arithmetic is removable, and charging
each selection rule for its own cost places every restriction mechanism on a
single axis, from full-score selection, bounded by two, to free selection,
which attains $N/k$. The measurements show that the bounded saving is
optimistic in this regime. Masked implementations execute none of it; gathered
implementations execute it and are slower, because compaction adds more traffic
than it removes whenever $kd > N$. Fused kernels remove most of that overhead
and are faster than the best dense implementation available to us only at one
retained peer (two at a single configuration), at head widths $16$ and $32$ and
without tensor cores; in TF32 and bf16, where dense attention gains three to
eight times, the advantage disappears from $384$ agents upward, and from four
retained peers upward the fused kernels are $1.3$ to $28$ times slower in fp32.

Selecting before scoring is where the saving becomes real, and the cost model
says in advance which selectors can deliver it. Once the attention over
pre-selected peers is fused it runs up to sixteen times faster than dense
attention; an all-pairs selector then dominates the cost and loses, while a
sub-quadratic selector reaches parity with dense attention at five hundred
agents on best-case timings. For designers, the practical guidance is to charge
the selector, to check $kd \le N$ before compacting, and to measure the kernel
that actually runs.
