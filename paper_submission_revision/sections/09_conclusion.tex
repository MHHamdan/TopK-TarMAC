The efficiency case for restricting inter-agent attention to each agent's
highest-scoring peers rests on an operation count, and the count is smaller than
it looks. Because the selection reads the scores, every pairwise interaction has
been evaluated before any is discarded, so only the aggregation is sparsifiable
and no choice of sparsity removes more than half of attention's arithmetic. That
bound is not a property of top-$k$ but of what the selector costs: written as
one
accounting in which a rule is charged for deciding, it places every restriction
mechanism on a single axis, and the mechanisms that obtain asymptotic savings
are exactly those whose selectors are cheap.

Measuring what executes shows the bounded saving to be optimistic twice over. An
implementation that applies the selection as a mask performs the dense
aggregation anyway, so it executes no saving while a count credits it with a
large one. An implementation that genuinely compacts the retained peers executes
the saving and is slower regardless, because compaction replaces multiplication
by zeros with the writing and re-reading of a gathered tensor, and at these
sizes time follows bytes. Gathering is byte-neutral only when the compacted
operand is no larger than the score row it avoids re-reading, which is a
criterion that can be checked before the code is written.

Whether the remaining penalty belongs to the algorithm or to the tools is a
question only kernels can answer, and writing them answered it in both
directions. Most of the measured penalty is the composition of general-purpose
operators; a fused kernel removes it, and at one retained peer the fused sparse
path is faster than any dense implementation we could obtain, including a search
over a hundred tilings of a dense kernel of our own. That is the whole of the
positive result. It holds at one retained peer, where the softmax is identically
one and nothing is being weighted; it holds only at the head widths where no
implementation we have reaches half the device's arithmetic rate, and disappears
where the vendor library does; and it is gone by four retained peers. At the
sparsity this literature uses, the fastest sparse implementation we could write
is more than an order of magnitude slower than dense attention.

The constructive half fares no better, and fails in a way we did not anticipate.
A selector that fixes the retained set before any score exists is genuinely
unbound by the ceiling, and the two we measured reduce the operation count by up
to two orders of magnitude. Neither is faster. One of them ranks a pairwise
matrix of its own and so reproduces the cost it was introduced to avoid, which
is the failure the accounting predicts once the selector is charged in the right
currency. The other does not, and still loses --- and it loses even with its
selector set aside and only the attention over the already-chosen peers timed.
Scoring $Nk$ chosen pairs and aggregating over $k$ of them is not cheaper than
scoring $N^2$ and aggregating over $N$, at these sizes, because the gathered
operand has lost the shape the hardware multiplies quickly. The arithmetic falls
by two orders of magnitude and the time does not move.

That is the finding we would put in front of a designer. An operation count
prices work in operations, and on this hardware, at these sizes, the price is
set by the shape the work is executed in. A successor method has to answer for
what its selector costs, for whether it reaches its decision without
materialising the structure it meant to avoid, and for whether what it leaves
behind can still be executed as a dense block. Only the first is visible in a
count. The third is why block-level selection succeeds at a hundred thousand
tokens and per-pair selection does not succeed at a few hundred agents: a
selected block is still a matrix multiply, and a handful of gathered peers is
not.
