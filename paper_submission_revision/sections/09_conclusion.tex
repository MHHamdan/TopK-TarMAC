We asked when an operation-count saving from sparse attention in multi-agent
communication becomes a saving in time, and answered by charging every
peer-selection rule for the cost of deciding. A rule that ranks scores must form
all of them, so at most half of the forward-pass multiply--accumulates are
removable; composed from library operators it is slower than dense attention
everywhere we measure, on four GPUs. Fused, it pays in fp32 with one retained
peer on every GPU we measured and with two on some, and in bf16 it is at best
near parity, because finding the retained set takes most of its time; where the
crossover falls depends on the retained count, the format and the GPU. Selecting
before scoring is where the saving can become real, and the selector then
decides the outcome; end to end, it did so on one of the four GPUs. At the
agent counts multi-agent methods use, the number of kernels a step launches
decides latency before either. A speedup claimed for sparse
communication therefore has to be measured, on the kernel that runs and against
the strongest dense baseline for the format.
