We asked when an operation-count saving from sparse attention in multi-agent
communication becomes a saving in time. A rule that selects peers by their
scores must form every score, so at most half of the forward-pass
multiply--accumulates are removable; charging other rules for their own
selection cost places them on one axis, from full-score selection, bounded by
two, to free selection, which attains $N/k$. In this regime even the bounded
saving is optimistic: masked implementations execute none of it, gathered ones
execute it and are slower because compaction adds traffic, and fused kernels win
only at one retained peer, at narrow heads and without tensor cores, losing
elsewhere to the cost of finding the retained set. Selecting before scoring is
where the saving can become real: attention over a pre-selected set runs up to
sixteen times faster than dense attention, and the selector then decides the
outcome. An operation count describes neither the cost of choosing peers nor the
cost of the kernel that runs; both have to be measured, on the same device and
against the strongest dense baseline available, before a speedup is claimed.
