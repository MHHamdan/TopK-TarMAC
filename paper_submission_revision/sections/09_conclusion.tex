We asked when an operation-count saving from sparse attention in multi-agent
communication becomes a saving in time, and used SCALE to charge every
peer-selection rule for the cost of deciding what to keep. A rule that ranks
attention scores must form all of them, so it can remove at most half of the
forward-pass multiply--accumulates. On the four GPUs measured it paid only as a
fused kernel retaining very few peers, in fp32; in bf16 it was at best near
parity, consistent with reduced precision accelerating the products but not
the comparisons of selection. A rule that selects before scoring can do
better, but only when its selector is cheap: end to end, the selector that was
cheapest at that size reached parity at $512$ agents on best-case timings, on one of the four GPUs. Below a hundred
agents, device time tracked the number of kernels a step launched more closely
than either mechanism. Section~\ref{sec:takeaways} lists the practical consequences.

These results concern the cost of sparse communication on one device; its value
for learning, or for bandwidth-limited deployment, is outside their scope. On
cost, the number of interactions retained was a poor guide to elapsed time.
The quantity that mattered was what has to be computed, or moved through
memory, to decide which interactions remain, and how many kernels it takes to
do so.
