Cooperative multi-agent systems increasingly pass learned messages between
agents, and attention is the mechanism they use to decide whose message matters
\citep{das2019tarmac,jiang2018atoc,jiang2018dgn}. It is a natural choice: the
weighting is learned, it adapts to the situation, and it needs no hand-designed
communication topology. It is also pairwise, so the channel costs quadratically
in the number of agents, and a large part of the literature on learned
communication is concerned with restricting who talks to whom.

Restricting communication can mean two different things, and the difference is
easy to lose. In a distributed deployment the objective is to reduce the
messages physically transmitted, and any restriction achieves that in proportion
to the links it removes. In the centralised inference that most of this
literature actually runs, the objective is to reduce the computation performed
on one device, and there the proportionality does not follow. This paper is
about the second. It asks what a particular and natural restriction --- keeping,
for each agent, the peers with the highest attention scores --- saves in local
computation, and whether the saving appears as elapsed time.

The efficiency case for that restriction is easy to state and easy to accept. If
dense aggregation combines messages from every peer and sparse aggregation
combines only a handful, then almost all of the aggregation disappears. What the
statement omits is where the handful comes from. Selecting peers by attention
score requires the scores, so by the time the selection can be made, every
pairwise interaction has been evaluated. Attention has two matrix products of
equal size, one producing the scores and one aggregating the values; sparsity of
this kind reaches only the second. The arithmetic that can be removed is
therefore at most half, whatever the sparsity, and the familiar fraction that
describes the aggregation term alone overstates the achievable saving by a
factor approaching two.

An operation count is a model of cost, and it can fail in more than one way. It
can describe a computation the implementation does not perform: applying the
selection as a mask on the dense attention matrix materialises the discarded
entries as zeros and multiplies them anyway, so the executed aggregation is
unchanged while a count based on the retained fraction reports a large saving.
And the quantity it describes may not govern the outcome: on hardware where time
tracks bytes moved rather than arithmetic performed, removing arithmetic from a
kernel that is already waiting on memory changes nothing. Both failures occur
here, and the second is the one that survives every correction we could make to
the first.

The question this paper answers empirically is therefore whether genuinely
executing the reduced aggregation makes score-then-select communication faster
in the agent-count regime multi-agent communication occupies. We answer it by
measuring six implementations of one function, verified equivalent at every
configuration whose latency we report, against dense attention through both a
library kernel and one we wrote ourselves.

The answer is conditional, and the condition is the contribution. Assembled from
general-purpose tensor operations, the sparse path is slower than dense
attention everywhere we measure, by up to an order of magnitude, and it moves
several times more memory traffic while performing less arithmetic. A fused
kernel that keeps the score row in registers and writes no intermediate removes
most of that penalty; at the very sparsest settings it makes the sparse path
genuinely faster than the best dense implementation available to us, by a margin
that stays inside the analytic ceiling. The advantage does not survive an
increase in the number of retained peers, because ranking candidates costs more
than aggregating over survivors saves, and it is gone well before the sparsity
levels this literature uses. A kernel can make selection cheap in the number
retained only by abandoning the compaction that made the aggregation sparse in
the first place, so on this hardware a score-then-select kernel can have sparse
arithmetic or cheap selection but not both.

What follows constructively is the mechanism the negative result identifies.
Selection that fixes the retained set before any score exists is not subject to
the ceiling, and its operation-count saving is unbounded in principle. Whether
it converts into time is a separate question, and we answer it for two selectors
the count treats similarly: choosing the nearest peers by distance, which is
what neighbourhood-based communication already does, and ordering agents along a
space-filling curve, which is asymptotically cheaper. They behave oppositely as
the agent count grows. The first saves an order of magnitude of arithmetic and
loses an order of magnitude of time, because it ranks a pairwise matrix of its
own; the second makes attention over the retained peers genuinely faster than
dense attention, and is held above parity only by the fixed cost of its own
sort. Escaping the operation-count bound is necessary, is not sufficient, and
does not by itself say which of two escaping selectors will be faster.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item We show that any rule selecting communication partners by their
    attention scores can remove at most half of attention's arithmetic, however
    sparse it is, and that the bound is the special case of a general accounting
    in which a selection rule is charged for what it costs to run. That
    accounting places existing multi-agent restriction mechanisms on one axis
    and separates those that escape the bound from those that inherit it
    (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).
  \item We measure what is actually executed, distinguishing masked, gathered
    and fused implementations of the same function, and find that the composed
    sparse path is slower than dense attention throughout the regime by a margin
    its operation count cannot predict, with memory traffic rather than
    arithmetic as the cause (Section~\ref{sec:measurement}).
  \item We write fused sparse kernels and a matched fused dense baseline, and
    thereby separate what the algorithm costs from what the implementation
    costs. Sparsity does become faster than dense attention, but only when one
    or two peers are retained, by less than the analytic ceiling permits, and
    never at the sparsity multi-agent communication uses
    (Section~\ref{sec:fused}).
  \item We instantiate the constructive half of the analysis with two selectors
    that fix the retained set before scoring, and show that removing the
    operation-count bound is necessary but not sufficient. A selector can save
    the arithmetic the accounting promises and cost an order of magnitude in
    time if it reaches its decision through a structure of the size it meant to
    avoid; and a selector that avoids that can make the attention itself faster
    than dense and still lose on its own fixed cost, which at these agent counts
    is comparable to the attention it displaces (Section~\ref{sec:prescore}).
\end{itemize}

A secondary pre-registered training study establishes that the architecture
being sparsified improves return, so that the sparsification comparisons are not
vacuous, and reports six under-powered null contrasts as what they are
(Section~\ref{sec:training}). Every computational claim concerns centralised
inference on a single device, not bandwidth-constrained distributed deployment;
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states the full envelope.
