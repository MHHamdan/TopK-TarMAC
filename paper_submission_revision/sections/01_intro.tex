Cooperative multi-agent systems increasingly exchange learned messages, and
attention is a common way of deciding whose message matters
\citep{das2019tarmac,jiang2018atoc,jiang2018dgn}.
Attention over $N$ agents is pairwise, so its cost grows quadratically, and
several methods restrict who attends to whom, citing communication or
computational cost among their reasons
\citep{jiang2018dgn,sun2020sparsecomm}. The computational
argument is an operation count: if each agent aggregates messages from $k$ peers
instead of $N-1$, the aggregation shrinks by a factor of about $N/k$.

Whether that saving becomes elapsed time depends on four quantities that an
operation count runs together. The first is \emph{what sparsity can remove at
all}. A rule that selects peers by their attention scores needs the scores, so
every pairwise score has been computed by the time the selection is made, and
only the aggregation can shrink. The second is \emph{what the implementation
executes}: masking the discarded weights to zero leaves the dense
multiplication in place, while gathering the retained values removes it and
writes new intermediates instead. The third is \emph{what deciding costs}. Every
rule spends something to identify the peers it keeps, and the usual count
charges nothing for it. The fourth is \emph{what the hardware spends its time
on}. At head width $16$, attention composed from library operators is
memory-bound, so an implementation that removes arithmetic while adding data
movement can become slower, and inside a fused kernel the comparisons of
selection can cost more than the arithmetic they remove.

These quantities are harder to amortise in multi-agent communication than in the
long-sequence attention where sparse attention is usually studied. A
communication step is a batch of many small attention problems, with tens to
hundreds of agents and narrow heads, so there is little skipped work per query
and no long sequence over which block-granular selection could amortise its
cost. We study centralised inference on a single device, the setting in which
most of the methods we cite are trained and evaluated in simulation. We make no
claim about bandwidth-constrained distributed deployment, such as multi-agent
orchestration across edge devices \citep{marliotp2026}, where reducing
transmitted messages is itself the objective. Since most of these methods are
evaluated with two to about a hundred agents (Section~\ref{sec:related}), the
question for multi-agent learning is whether the computational case for sparse
communication holds at those sizes, and how far above them it begins to.

We introduce \emph{SCALE} (Selector-Cost Accounting for Latency Evaluation), an
accounting that charges every peer-selection rule for the cost of deciding what
to keep (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}). It bounds the first quantity analytically,
makes the third an explicit term, and gives a screen for the fourth that can be applied before any
kernel is written. The experiments measure the second and fourth and test the
bounds on the first and third.

Most multi-agent methods that restrict communication choose peers before
scoring them, from positions, per-agent gates or a separate network
(Section~\ref{sec:related}). \emph{Score-then-select}, which ranks the attention
scores themselves, is uncommon in this literature, and the cost argument made for
it is a reduction in complexity \citep{sun2020sparsecomm}; no measured speedup
has been claimed for it. In SCALE it is the limiting case, the rule whose decision
requires every score.

The evidence comes from three comparisons (Figure~\ref{fig:methodology}).
Masked, gathered and fused implementations of one top-$k$ function show whether
the counted saving is executed and whether it becomes time. A fixed-set
comparison hands the exact retained set to a fused kernel at no cost, which
separates finding the retained peers from attending over them. Three selectors
that choose peers before scoring, spanning the non-trivial classes of the
accounting,
test the case in which the score term itself can shrink. The headline
measurements are repeated on three further NVIDIA GPUs, four in all across three
generations.

\paragraph{Summary of findings.} Under the assumptions of Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling}, a score-then-select
rule can remove at most half of the forward-pass attention
multiply--accumulates, a limit noted before in a
memory-access model \citep{chen2023dfss}. Composed from library operators, such
a rule is slower than dense attention at every agent count and sparsity we
measure on four GPUs, the gathered form by up to thirty-seven times, because of
memory traffic. In fp32, at head widths $16$ and $32$ and a batch of $256$,
fused kernels are faster than dense attention only when very few peers are
retained: one on every GPU we measured, and two or four only
on some, depending on the kernel and the GPU. From $384$ agents upward most of
their time goes to finding the retained set. In bf16, which accelerates the
matrix products but not the comparisons of selection, the fused kernel that
streams the selection is at most near parity, and only on one GPU. Rules that select before scoring escape the bound,
and attention over a set chosen before scoring is several times faster than dense
attention on every GPU we measured, but the whole path pays only if the selector
is cheap in absolute terms; in our measurements one selector reached parity,
on best-case timings, on one GPU and at the largest agent count. Below a hundred agents, where most of
the methods we cite are evaluated, device time follows the number of kernels a
communication step issues more closely than its operation count.
Section~\ref{sec:measurement} gives the numbers and the conditions under which
each holds.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{An accounting.} SCALE (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}) charges every
    peer-selection rule for deciding what to keep and sorts mechanisms into four
    asymptotic classes (Proposition~\ref{prop:escape},
    Corollary~\ref{cor:taxonomy}). Its limiting case, score-then-select, has a
    closed-form multiply--accumulate limit for the forward and backward passes
    (Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling}), and a byte criterion, $kd \le N$, screens
    compaction before implementation.
  \item \textbf{A measurement protocol} for microsecond-scale attention kernels
    (Section~\ref{sec:measurement}). Implementations of one function, verified
    equivalent, are timed interleaved against the better of a vendor library and
    a dense kernel searched over the sparse kernels' parameter space, with
    FlexAttention added in the reduced-precision comparison. A fixed-set
    comparison separates finding the retained peers from using them, and a
    checklist records eight errors that each produced a plausible but spurious
    result (Section~\ref{sec:checklist}).
  \item \textbf{An empirical characterisation}, on four GPUs across three
    generations (Sections~\ref{sec:fused}--\ref{sec:smalln},
    Appendix~\ref{app:portability}), of when the arithmetic removed by exact
    top-$k$ attention between agents becomes wall-clock time, separating the
    cost of finding the retained set from the cost of attending over it.
\end{itemize}
