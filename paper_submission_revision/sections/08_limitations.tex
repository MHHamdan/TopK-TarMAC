\subsection{Implications for designing sparse communication}
\label{sec:takeaways}

The measurements locate the cost of sparse communication in deciding which
interactions to keep, a quantity an operation count leaves out. The practices
below follow from them, and each holds inside the measured envelope
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Charge the selector.} Whether selecting before scoring paid end
    to end depended on the selector's absolute cost, and at the agent counts
    measured its asymptotic class did not fix the ordering
    (Section~\ref{sec:prescore}).
  \item \textbf{Separate scoring from aggregation.} A rule that ranks scores
    cannot remove them, and in our fused kernels finding the retained set took
    most of the time (Section~\ref{sec:fused}).
  \item \textbf{Check what the implementation executes and moves.} A masked
    implementation executes none of the counted saving
    (Section~\ref{sec:composed}). Before compacting, $kd \le N$ is a screen under
    a simplified byte model (Section~\ref{sec:roofline}); in our measurements the
    composed path was slower on both sides of it (Table~\ref{tab:headline},
    Figure~\ref{fig:ksweep}).
  \item \textbf{Compare against the strongest dense kernel for the format.} In
    bf16 the score-then-select advantage was at best near parity at a single
    retained peer, and absent on three of the four GPUs
    (Section~\ref{sec:precision}).
  \item \textbf{Count kernels at small agent counts.} Below a hundred agents,
    device time tracked the number of kernels launched more closely than the
    operation count (Section~\ref{sec:smalln}).
  \item \textbf{Keep attention-stage and end-to-end figures apart.} A sixteenfold
    attention-stage speedup at $512$ agents on the Blackwell card and a $2\%$
    spread across implementations in a full training iteration at six to
    twenty-four agents are both true of this system
    (Sections~\ref{sec:prescore} and~\ref{sec:latency}).
  \item \textbf{Re-measure on the target GPU.} On the three other GPUs we
    measured, the streaming kernel's crossover in fp32 and bf16 and the
    pre-score crossover were narrower than on the Blackwell card
    (Appendix~\ref{app:portability}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Setting.} Every cost result concerns centralised inference on a
single device. Bandwidth-constrained deployment is outside our scope
(Section~\ref{sec:intro}), and there top-$k$ selection may well be the right
design. The analysis is single-round
and single-head. The operation-count ratio is invariant to both, but $H$ heads
of width $d/H$ change the per-head width, and the sign of the best sparse
implementation's advantage over dense attention changes with head width
(Appendix~\ref{sec:sensitivity}). Every timing claim concerns the forward pass
of the attention core unless stated otherwise; the backward pass is reported
only for the differentiable composed implementations, and the complete MAPPO
iteration only at six to twenty-four agents (Appendix~\ref{app:backward}).

\paragraph{Measured envelope.} The full sweep was measured on one GPU (NVIDIA
RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell, sm\_120) with one x86\_64 host CPU, under PyTorch 2.11,
CUDA 12.8 and Triton 3.6, at head widths $16$ to $512$, $N \le 512$ ($1024$ for
the streaming kernel), batch $32$ to $1024$, and in fp32, TF32 and bf16. The
headline measurements (the composed and fused crossovers, the fixed-set split,
the streaming kernel in all three formats, the pre-score path, small $N$ and
batch to $4096$) were repeated on an A100, an H100 and an H200
(Appendix~\ref{app:portability}). On all four GPUs the composed path is slower
than dense attention and the resident kernel's one-peer crossover recurs. The
streaming and pre-score crossovers are narrower on the other three than on the
Blackwell card, so the figures in the main text are best cases across the
devices we measured. We did not measure CPUs or non-NVIDIA accelerators.

\paragraph{Timing.} Both Blackwell cards of the host, used for the main sweep
and the fixed-set sweep, were shared with other tenants throughout, and their
clocks could not be locked. Every comparative claim is therefore a ratio between
implementations timed in the same interleaved sweep, and below $192$ agents only
the CUDA-graph device times of Section~\ref{sec:smalln} are used. The A100, H100
and H200 were each allocated exclusively, so the orderings that recur on them
(Appendix~\ref{app:portability}) are not artefacts of contention on the
Blackwell card. Timing a single call with device events measures host launch
latency whenever the host is slower than the kernel. Our kernels run for $5$ to
$140$\,\textmu s, and during the fixed-set sweep the host launch path was slow
enough that the eager figure for the fixed-set kernel was $1.7$ to $3.4$ times its graph-replay figure. The score-then-select ratios agreed under both
protocols, but the smallest device times we report are not what an eager
deployment on a slower host would see.

\paragraph{Kernels.} Our kernels are not optimal. The resident-row kernels
admit only three tilings above $192$ agents against $28$ to $106$ for the dense kernel, so their efficiency figures are lower bounds and the negative results
at wide heads are weakly supported. The streaming kernel removes that
constraint, and in fp32 on the Blackwell card it moves the boundary from one
retained peer to between two and four. It was searched at head widths $16$ and
$32$ only, and its merge is a sequence of comparisons that a better selection
network might shorten, so the boundary may move further; it cannot move past the
score product, about half of dense attention. The composed baseline's selection
is \texttt{torch.topk} as PyTorch 2.11 implements it. We did not benchmark other
exact top-$k$ designs, such as sample-guided or other radix selections, nor
amortised or cross-layer indexers; attention over a given set
(Table~\ref{tab:fixedset}) bounds what any of them could recover. The retained
count is a compile-time constant and $N$ is uniform within a launch, so a
captured CUDA graph serves one $(B, N, k)$; per-row $k$ or ragged agent counts
would need a length argument per row, which we have not written.

\paragraph{Selectors and learning.} We did not measure how changes to the
retained set under reduced precision affect training. In the formats and configurations
we report, bf16 changed the retained peers in at most \PortBfMismatchMax{} of
\PortBfRowsM{} million rows on any GPU, and TF32 configurations that changed
more than $0.1\%$ of rows are excluded. The pre-score selectors attend to a
different peer set than top-$k$ does (Section~\ref{sec:prescore}). Their one
quality check, Z-order in the learning study (Appendix~\ref{sec:training}), is
descriptive, at six agents and five seeds. They are composed from library
operators, so the selector costs we report are upper bounds, and their envelope
is one head width, one batch and fp32, with uniformly placed agents. Finally,
the learning study has five seeds per variant; it shows that the communicating
architecture improves return and cannot rank sparsification variants.
