\paragraph{What a designer can take from this.} Each of the following is a
conclusion from the measurements above, inside their envelope, rather than a
general law. Charge the selector: its cost decides whether pre-scoring pays, and
an asymptotic class does not fix the ordering at the agent counts measured.
Separate scoring from aggregation: a rule that ranks scores cannot remove them,
and in our fused kernels finding the retained set was most of the time. Check
what the implementation executes: a masked implementation executes none of the
counted saving. Check traffic before compacting, with $kd \le N$ as a necessary
screen. Compare against the strongest dense kernel available for the format,
including reduced precision, which removed the one positive score-then-select
result. And keep attention-stage and end-to-end figures apart: a sixteenfold
attention-stage speedup and a $2\%$ spread across implementations in a full
training iteration are both true of this system.

Appendix~\ref{sec:pitfalls} lists eight measurement errors that produced plausible but spurious results during this study before they were caught, with the practice that corrected each.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Every cost result concerns centralised inference on a
single device; the paper makes no claim about bandwidth-constrained distributed
deployment, where reducing transmitted messages is itself the objective and
top-$k$ selection may well be the right design. The analysis is single-round and single-head; the operation-count ratio is invariant to both, but $H$ heads of width $d/H$ change the per-head width, along which our timing result changes sign.

The measured envelope is one GPU architecture (NVIDIA Blackwell, sm\_120), one
x86\_64 CPU, PyTorch 2.11 with CUDA 12.8 and Triton 3.6, head widths $16$ to
$512$, $N \le 512$, batch $32$ to $1024$, and fp32, TF32 and bf16. We could not
replicate on a second GPU generation, because no other was available to us; the
fixed-set sweep ran on the second card of the same model. Both cards were shared
with other tenants throughout, and clocks could not be locked; every claim except the tiling search's efficiency figures is therefore a ratio between implementations timed in the same interleaved sweep, and no claim is read off an agent count below $192$. Timing a single call with
device events measures host launch latency whenever the host is slower than the
kernel; our kernels run for $5$ to $140$\,\textmu s, and during the fixed-set
sweep the host launch path was slow enough that the eager figure for the fixed-set kernel was $1.7$ to $3.4$ times its graph-replay figure. The score-then-select
ratios agreed under both protocols, but the smallest device times we report are
not what an eager deployment on a slower host would see. 

Our kernels are not optimal. The sparse kernels hold the whole score row of a
query on chip, which caps them at $N \le 1024$ and admits only three tilings
above $192$ agents against $28$ to $106$ for the dense kernel; a streaming exact
top-$k$ that keeps a running candidate set would not have that constraint, and
we did not write one. The sparse efficiency figures are therefore lower bounds,
and the negative results at wide heads are weakly supported. The fused
crossover at one retained peer is provisional in the other direction, and
Section~\ref{sec:precision} shows how: a dense kernel that uses the tensor cores
removes it from $384$ agents upward. The pre-score selectors attend to a
different peer set than top-$k$ does, so their comparison is of cost and not of
quality; their selectors are composed from library operators, so the selector
costs we report are upper bounds; and their envelope is one head width, one
batch and fp32, with uniformly placed agents. Finally, the learning study
(Appendix~\ref{sec:training}) has five seeds per variant; it shows that the
communicating architecture improves return and cannot rank sparsification
variants.
