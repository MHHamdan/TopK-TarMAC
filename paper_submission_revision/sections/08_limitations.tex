Every cost result here concerns centralised inference on a single device: the
latency and memory of executing the communication module in one process that
holds all agents' observations. It is not a claim about bandwidth-constrained
distributed deployment, where the objective is to reduce messages physically
transmitted and top-$k$ sparsification may well be correct even though it does
not reduce local computation. Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} says nothing about
transmitted bytes, and neither do the measurements. The analysis is also
single-round and single-head. Assumptions A1 and A3 show that the
\emph{operation-count} ratio is invariant to rounds and heads, but this paper's
whole argument is that the operation-count ratio does not determine time, so
that invariance cannot be used to extend a timing result. It should not be: $H$
heads of width $d/H$ is a change in per-head width, and per-head width is the
axis along which our timing result changes sign.

The measured envelope is one GPU architecture (NVIDIA Blackwell, sm\_120), one
x86\_64 CPU, PyTorch 2.11 with CUDA 12.8 and Triton 3.6, input embedding width
$64$, head width $d \in \{16,\dots,512\}$, $N \le 512$, batch $32$--$1024$,
\texttt{fp32} and \texttt{bf16}, and eager, compiled and CUDA-graph execution.
The crossover of Section~\ref{sec:fused} is narrower still: it appears only at
$d \in \{16,32\}$, only at $N \ge 192$, and only in \texttt{fp32}, which is the
dtype in which dense attention is least advantaged. We could not replicate on a
second GPU generation, because the host has two cards of the same model and the
hardware that produced this project's earlier runs is gone. Both cards were
shared with other tenants throughout. That is why every claim is stated as a
ratio between implementations timed in the same interleaved sweep, why the
headline sweep was repeated in full, and why no claim is read off an agent count
below $192$, where the repeat does not reproduce.

Our sparse implementations are not optimal and neither is our dense baseline. We
searched the same parameter space for both under one admissibility filter, and
report what fraction of the device's measured matrix-multiply rate each one
reaches, because matched authorship is a weaker condition than matched efficiency
and only the second identifies the algorithm. The budgets the filter admits are
not equal --- three configurations for the sparse kernel above $192$ agents
against twenty-eight to a hundred and six for the dense one, for the structural
reason Section~\ref{sec:fused} gives --- so the sparse figure is a lower bound
on what a kernel of its shape could reach, and the negative entries at the wider
head widths are weakly supported. At the head width where the crossover lives no
implementation available to us --- the vendor library, our own kernel, or any
configuration in the search --- exceeds $44\%$ of that rate, and the sparse
kernel reaches $29\%$. The comparison is therefore between a better dense kernel
and a worse sparse one, which bounds the direction of any residual bias but not
its size: a dense kernel substantially closer to the machine's limit at this
head
width would remove the crossover, and we cannot rule one out. Separately, the
sparse kernels select by argmax rounds or by bisection rather than by a radix or
bitonic select, they are forward-only, and they hold the whole score row in
registers, which caps them at $N \le 1024$ and limits how many queries can be in
flight at the largest sizes. What no kernel removes is the requirement to form
and rank the full score matrix.

The learning study is secondary and bounded by its power. Five seeds per
implementation gives a minimum attainable two-sided permutation $p$ of $0.0079$
against a first Holm threshold of $0.0083$, so a first rejection requires one
implementation's seeds to lie entirely above the other's. Resolving the effect
the primary comparison suggests would need roughly $45$ seeds per
implementation, or $70$ at the family-corrected level, from a
normal-approximation formula that guides order of magnitude rather than
describing the exact test we run. That was a budget allocation and not an
unaffordable experiment: the $60$-run study consumed $31.9$ GPU-hours and a
properly powered primary comparison would have cost about $37.9$ more. Its
positive controls do not isolate the learned channel, for reasons given in
Section~\ref{sec:training}, and one of them sits at an agent count where the
selection mechanism is degenerate. Readers should treat the study as evidence
that the architecture is not inert, and as no evidence at all about the six
comparisons it could not resolve.

Finally, the pre-score selectors of Section~\ref{sec:prescore} demonstrate a
cost structure and not a method. They attend to a different peer set than
top-$k$ does, so the comparison is of cost and not of quality, and we make no
claim that spatial neighbours carry the information attention would have
selected. Their selectors are also composed from library operators, so the
constant we measure for them is an upper bound in the same way the composed
sparse path's was before we fused it; how far a fused selector could fall is the
obvious next measurement and one we did not make.
