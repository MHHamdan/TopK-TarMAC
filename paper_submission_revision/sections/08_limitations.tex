\subsection{What a designer can take from this}
\label{sec:takeaways}

Each of the following is a
conclusion from the measurements above, inside their envelope, rather than a
general law. Charge the selector: its cost decides whether pre-scoring pays, and
an asymptotic class does not fix the ordering at the agent counts measured.
Separate scoring from aggregation: a rule that ranks scores cannot remove them,
and in our fused kernels finding the retained set was most of the time. Check
what the implementation executes: a masked implementation executes none of the
counted saving. Check traffic before compacting, with $kd \le N$ as a necessary
screen. Compare against the strongest dense kernel available for the format,
including reduced precision, which in bf16 confined the score-then-select
advantage to a single retained peer. Count kernels at small agent counts, where
the number launched orders latency. And keep attention-stage and end-to-end figures apart: a sixteenfold
attention-stage speedup and a $2\%$ spread across implementations in a full
training iteration are both true of this system.

\subsection{A checklist for benchmarking small kernels}
\label{sec:checklist}

Attention over tens to hundreds of agents runs for microseconds, on a device
that is often shared, against library kernels tuned for other shapes. Each of
those conditions produced at least one error in this study that yielded a
plausible number, and Table~\ref{tab:pitfalls} lists them with the practice that
prevents each. Two of the eight deserve emphasis because nothing in the
resulting numbers betrays them: a masked implementation benchmarked as sparse
executes none of the counted saving, and a hand-written sparse kernel timed
against a vendor's dense kernel measures authorship as well as sparsity. The
first is caught by counting the operations the running program issues, the
second by searching both kernels over one parameter space and reporting the
fraction of the machine each reaches. Appendix~\ref{sec:pitfalls} describes each
entry and how it was found.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

Every cost result concerns centralised inference on a
single device; the paper makes no claim about bandwidth-constrained distributed
deployment, where reducing transmitted messages is itself the objective and
top-$k$ selection may well be the right design. The analysis is single-round and single-head; the operation-count ratio is invariant to both, but $H$ heads of width $d/H$ change the per-head width, along which our timing result changes sign.

The measured envelope is one GPU architecture (NVIDIA Blackwell, sm\_120), one
x86\_64 CPU, PyTorch 2.11 with CUDA 12.8 and Triton 3.6, head widths $16$ to
$512$, $N \le 512$ ($1024$ for the streaming kernel), batch $32$ to $1024$, and fp32, TF32 and bf16. We could not
replicate on a second GPU generation, because no other was available to us; the
fixed-set sweep ran on the second card of the same model. Both cards were shared
with other tenants throughout, and clocks could not be locked; every claim except the tiling search's efficiency figures is therefore a ratio between implementations timed in the same interleaved sweep, and below $192$ agents only the CUDA-graph device times of Section~\ref{sec:smalln} are used. Timing a single call with
device events measures host launch latency whenever the host is slower than the
kernel; our kernels run for $5$ to $140$\,\textmu s, and during the fixed-set
sweep the host launch path was slow enough that the eager figure for the fixed-set kernel was $1.7$ to $3.4$ times its graph-replay figure. The score-then-select
ratios agreed under both protocols, but the smallest device times we report are
not what an eager deployment on a slower host would see. 

Our kernels are not optimal. The resident-row kernels admit only three tilings
above $192$ agents against $28$ to $106$ for the dense kernel, so their
efficiency figures are lower bounds and the negative results at wide heads are
weakly supported. The streaming kernel removes that constraint and moved the
boundary from one retained peer to between two and four; it was searched at head
widths $16$ and $32$ only, and its merge is a sequence of comparisons that a
better selection network might shorten, so the boundary may move further. It
cannot move past the score product, about half of dense attention. The pre-score selectors attend to a
different peer set than top-$k$ does, so their comparison is of cost; the one
quality check, Z-order in the learning study (Appendix~\ref{sec:training}), is
descriptive at six agents and five seeds; their selectors are composed from library operators, so the selector
costs we report are upper bounds; and their envelope is one head width, one
batch and fp32, with uniformly placed agents. Finally, the learning study
(Appendix~\ref{sec:training}) has five seeds per variant; it shows that the
communicating architecture improves return and cannot rank sparsification
variants.
