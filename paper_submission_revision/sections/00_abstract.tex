Sparse attention between agents is often motivated by the computation it
removes: if each agent aggregates messages from a few peers instead of all of
them, the operation count falls. That argument leaves out what it costs to
choose those peers. We introduce SCALE (Selector-Cost Accounting for Latency
Evaluation), which charges each peer-selection rule for the cost of deciding
what to keep, and use it to measure when the saving becomes elapsed time on four
NVIDIA GPUs (A100, H100, H200 and an RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell). A rule that ranks
attention scores must compute all of them first, so with keys and values of
equal width it removes at most half of the forward-pass multiply--accumulates
at any sparsity. Whether that half becomes time depends on the implementation
and the hardware; the latency ratios below are ratios of minimum times over
interleaved repetitions. Composed from library operators, score-then-select attention
is slower than dense attention at every configuration we measure on all four
GPUs, by up to 37 times, because forming, ranking and compacting the scores
moves far more data than dense attention does. A fused kernel that streams the
selection is faster than dense attention in fp32 with one retained peer on all
four GPUs, at head widths 16 and 32 and a batch of 256. With two it is faster on the Blackwell card and the
A100 and in most configurations on the H100 and H200, and with four it is within 15\% of parity on the
Blackwell card and slower on the rest. From 384 agents upward at least 85\% of
its time goes to finding the retained set, that is, to forming and ranking every
score; only the ranking can grow with the retained count. In bf16 the same kernel is near parity with one retained peer on the
Blackwell card (0.85 to 1.06 times dense) and slower than dense attention on the
other three. Rules that select peers before scoring them escape the ceiling, to
the extent that their selector is cheap. Once the set is known, attention over
two retained peers is up to 16 times faster than dense attention on the
Blackwell card and 4 to 7 times on the others; end to end, however, only the
selector that is cheapest at that size reaches parity, at 512 agents, on best-case timings
and on the Blackwell card only. Below 100 agents, where multi-agent methods are
usually evaluated, device time on all four GPUs follows the number of kernels a
step issues more closely than its operation count.
