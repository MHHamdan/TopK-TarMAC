Attention-based communication between agents is routinely sparsified on the
grounds that attending to a few peers rather than to all of them removes most of
the work, and the case is almost always made by counting operations rather than
by measuring execution. We show that the count is smaller than it appears, and
that what it buys in time is smaller still. When an agent's partners are chosen
by their attention scores, every score has already been computed before any can
be discarded, so only the aggregation is removable and no choice of sparsity can
remove more than half the arithmetic of attention. Whether even that half
becomes time depends on the implementation to a degree an operation count cannot
see. Applying the selection as a mask leaves the dense aggregation in place and
executes no saving at all; a genuinely sparse implementation assembled from
general-purpose tensor operations moves several times more data than dense
attention despite performing less arithmetic, and runs an order of magnitude
slower. Writing a fused kernel that keeps every intermediate on chip removes
most of that penalty, and where a single peer is retained it makes the sparse
path faster than any dense implementation we could obtain. That is the only
regime in which it does, it holds only at narrow head widths where nothing we
tried reaches half the machine's arithmetic rate, and retaining one peer is not
a communication design. At every sparsity the field actually uses, the fastest
sparse implementation we could construct remains an order of magnitude slower,
because ranking the candidates costs more than aggregating over the survivors
saves. Deciding what to attend to before the scores exist removes the bound
entirely, and there the picture reverses: with a selector that avoids a
quadratic intermediate and an attention kernel written for the resulting
access pattern, attention over pre-chosen peers is up to eighteen times faster
than dense attention and the whole path overtakes it at a few hundred agents.
Neither condition suffices alone, and an operation count expresses neither.
