Sparse attention in multi-agent communication is commonly motivated by counting
the operations it removes. For rules that select partners by their attention
scores, the count is bounded and what it buys in time is smaller still. Because
every score is computed before any can be discarded, only the aggregation is
removable, so no such rule can remove more than half of the arithmetic of an
attention forward pass at any sparsity. Whether even that half becomes time depends on the
implementation. Applying the selection as a mask leaves the dense aggregation in
place and saves nothing, while a genuinely sparse implementation moves more data
than dense attention despite less arithmetic. Fused kernels remove most of that
overhead and expose the algorithm: retaining a single peer is faster than the
best dense implementation we could obtain, but that case is nearly degenerate,
and from four retained peers upward the sparse path is 1.3 to 28 times slower.
Deciding what to attend to before any score exists lifts the bound, and there
the selector's own cost decides the outcome. A selector that ranks all pairs in
a cheap feature space cannot beat a constant however sparse the attention
becomes; a sub-quadratic one is unbounded, and behind a fused kernel its
attention stage runs up to sixteen times faster than dense, with the complete
path reaching parity at five hundred agents on best-case timings. All timings
are from one GPU architecture; the operation-count results are
hardware-independent.
