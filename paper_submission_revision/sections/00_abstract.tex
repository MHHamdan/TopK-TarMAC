Sparse attention in multi-agent communication is usually justified by the
operations it removes. We charge each peer-selection rule for what deciding
costs, and measure on four NVIDIA GPUs (A100, H100, H200 and an RTX PRO 6000
Blackwell) when the saving becomes elapsed time.
First, a rule that ranks attention scores must compute all of them, so it
removes at most half of the forward-pass multiply--accumulates at any sparsity;
rules that select before scoring escape this ceiling by as much as their
selector is cheap. Second, composed from library operators, score-then-select
attention is slower than dense attention at every configuration we measure on
all four GPUs, by up to 37 times, because forming, ranking and compacting the
scores moves far more data than dense attention does. A fused kernel that
streams the selection is faster than dense attention in fp32 with one retained
peer on all four GPUs; with two it is faster on two of them and in most
configurations on the others, and with four it is within 15\% of parity on the
Blackwell card and slower on the rest. From 384 agents upward at least 85\% of
its time is spent finding the retained set, so selection, not arithmetic, sets
the crossover. In bf16 it is near parity with one retained peer on the
Blackwell card (0.85 to 1.06 times dense) and slower than dense attention on the
other three. Third, once the set is known without scoring, attention over it
is up to 16 times faster than dense attention on the Blackwell card and 4 to 7
times on the others, but the selector decides the outcome: of three
selectors, only the cheapest reaches parity end to end, at 512 agents, on
best-case timings and on the Blackwell card only. Below 100 agents, where
multi-agent methods are usually evaluated, the number of kernels a step
launches orders latency on all four GPUs, and its operation count does not.
