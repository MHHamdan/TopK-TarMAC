Sparse attention in multi-agent communication is routinely justified by counting
the operations it removes. For rules that select partners by their attention
scores, the count is bounded and what it buys in time is smaller still. Because every score is computed before any can be discarded, only the
aggregation is removable, so no such rule can remove more than half of
attention's arithmetic at any sparsity. Whether even that half becomes time
depends on the implementation. Applying the selection as a mask leaves the dense
aggregation in place and saves nothing; a genuinely sparse one moves more data than
dense attention despite less arithmetic. Fused kernels remove most of that
overhead and expose the algorithm: retaining a single peer is faster than the
best dense implementation we could obtain, but that case is degenerate, and at
the moderate retained sets we test sparsity is an order of magnitude slower.
Deciding what to attend to before any score exists lifts the bound, and there
the selector's own cost decides the outcome. A selector that ranks all pairs in
a cheap feature space cannot beat a constant however sparse the attention
becomes; a sub-quadratic one is unbounded, and behind a fused kernel its
attention stage runs up to sixteen times faster than dense, and on best-case
timings the complete path reaches parity at five hundred agents. Count the
selector, and measure the kernel that runs.
