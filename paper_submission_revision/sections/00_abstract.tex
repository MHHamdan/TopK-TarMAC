Attention-based communication between agents is routinely sparsified on the
grounds that attending to a few peers removes most of the work, and the case is
usually made by counting operations rather than by measuring execution. We
show that the count is smaller than it appears, and that what it buys in time is
smaller still. When partners are chosen by their attention scores, every score
has been computed before any can be discarded, so only the aggregation is
removable and no sparsity can remove more than half of attention's arithmetic.
Whether even that half becomes time depends on the implementation, to a degree
no count can see. Applying the selection as a mask leaves the dense
aggregation in place and saves nothing; a genuinely sparse implementation built
from general-purpose tensor operations moves several times more data despite
less arithmetic, and runs an order of magnitude slower. A fused
kernel that keeps every intermediate on chip removes most of that penalty, and
at a single retained peer it makes the sparse path faster than any dense
implementation we could obtain. That is the only regime in which it does, at
narrow head widths only, and one peer is not a communication design. At
every sparsity the field actually uses, the fastest sparse implementation we
could construct is an order of magnitude slower, because ranking candidates
costs more than aggregating over the survivors saves. Deciding what to attend to
before the scores exist removes the bound, and there the picture reverses: with
a selector that avoids a quadratic intermediate and an attention kernel written
for the resulting access pattern, attention over pre-chosen peers is up to
seventeen times faster than dense, and at the largest agent count we measure
the whole path overtakes it. Neither condition suffices alone, and an operation count
expresses neither.
