\subsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

We time the communication module in isolation, with the training loop removed.
The protocol is stated in full because several of its provisions were forced by
defects that produced spurious results first (Section~\ref{sec:pitfalls}).

Timing uses on-device CUDA events with an explicit synchronisation on the
correct device. Any diagnostic that calls \texttt{.item()} is disabled, because
such a call synchronises the device \emph{inside} the timed forward pass. Arms
are timed interleaved repetition by repetition rather than in sequential
per-arm blocks, so that interference from other tenants lands on all arms at
once and the ratios between them remain paired; on this shared host the
sequential design produced swings above $100\%$ between identical
configurations, which interleaving reduced to typically under $2\%$. We report
$\min$ over samples as the primary statistic, since contention can only add
time, and give median, IQR and inter-run spread in
Appendix~\ref{app:dispersion} together with the sensitivity of every headline
ratio to that choice. GPU clocks could not be locked --- the account lacks the
privilege. Executed FLOPs are counted with \texttt{torch.utils.flop\_counter}
so that latency is always reported beside the arithmetic actually performed.

Unless stated otherwise: PyTorch 2.11 / CUDA 12.8, \texttt{fp32}, input
embedding width $64$, head width $d{=}16$, batch $256$, $k = 25\%$ of peers,
and an idle device.

Table~\ref{tab:headline} is the central measurement. The gathered
implementation does execute fewer multiply--accumulates than unfused dense ---
$3553$ against $5167$ MFLOP at $N{=}512$, a $31\%$ reduction consistent with
Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} --- and it is nonetheless $3.53\times$ slower
than unfused dense and $16.29\times$ slower than the fused kernel. Correcting
the implementation helps without being sufficient: replacing the masked variant
with the gathered one improves the ratio against unfused dense from
$\approx1.45\times$ to $\approx1.23\times$ for $N \le 48$. The improvement does
not persist --- at $N{=}96$ and $N{=}192$ the gathered path is slower than the
masked one it corrects ($0.224$ vs $0.214$; $0.771$ vs $0.681$), because the
gather's traffic grows faster than the masked path's wasted arithmetic.

The two ends of this table are governed by different mechanisms, and the
distinction matters for how far the numbers generalise. For $N \le 96$ the
unfused dense arm is nearly flat at $\approx0.12$\,ms while its work grows
$256$-fold: these cells are bound by kernel launch count, not by bandwidth or
arithmetic, and the $1.17$--$1.23\times$ ratios there reflect how many kernels
each arm issues. Ratios in this regime are sensitive to how many arms share a
timing group, and we measure a $5\times$ disagreement between two of our own
sweeps on the same nominal cell for this reason
(Section~\ref{sec:pitfalls}, P6). From $N{=}192$ upward the working set exceeds
L2, traffic is real, and the roofline account of Section~\ref{sec:roofline}
applies.

\paragraph{Robustness.} The ordering is unchanged under \texttt{torch.compile}
and CUDA-graph capture --- gathered latency moves by $1.9\%$, though individual
ratios move by up to $27\%$ at $N{=}12$ where launch effects dominate --- and at
batch $4096$, where the largest cells become FLOP-bound. In \texttt{bf16} the
gap is wider, reaching $19.7\times$ the fused kernel at $N{=}512$. We note that
FlashAttention never served any arm: our fused baseline passes an additive
float mask, which excludes that backend at every dtype, so the fused numbers
throughout are PyTorch's memory-efficient CUTLASS kernel. A FlashAttention
baseline reached through a Boolean mask would be a stronger comparison than the
one we report, not a weaker one.

\paragraph{Selection is not tuned to its floor.} Our gathered path calls
\texttt{torch.topk} with its default \texttt{sorted=True}. The ordering of the
$k$ retained entries is irrelevant here, since the softmax and the gather are
permuted consistently by the same indices, so \texttt{sorted=False} computes
the identical function and is strictly cheaper. Re-timing under the protocol
above (agreement to $5.4\times10^{-7}$; inter-run spread $\le 2.2\%$),
selection falls by up to $35\%$ and the whole gathered arm by $5$--$24\%$,
moving the ratio against unfused dense at $N{=}192$ from $4.97$ to $3.79$ and
at $N{=}512$ from $3.61$ to $3.36$ in that measurement, with the ordering
unchanged at every $N$. The campaign in this section predates that correction,
so every ratio here should be read as an upper bound on the penalty in that
respect. We flag it rather than quietly re-running because it makes the paper's
own point: a sparse path has more places to leave performance on the floor than
a dense one, and an operation count sees none of them.

\subsection{Is there any $(d, B, N, k)$ where sparsity wins?}
\label{sec:surface}

Reporting isolated favourable cells as anomalies is not a characterisation. We
swept head width $d \in \{32,64,128,256\}$ against batch $\in
\{32,256,4096\}$ and $N \in \{12,48,192,512\}$, and for each cell took the
\emph{best} $k$ for the gather path, which is the most favourable operating
point available to it.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{figures/fig2_crossover.pdf}
\caption{Crossover surface: best-$k$ gathered latency divided by \emph{unfused}
dense, on GPU in \texttt{fp32} eager mode. Every one of the $46$ measured cells
exceeds $1$. The ratio falls monotonically in head width at $N{=}512$
($2.43 \to 2.46 \to 2.12 \to 1.71$ at $B{=}256$), so the surface is flat in
batch but not in $d$; the sweep ends at $d{=}256$. Hatched cells exceeded the
memory budget. Against the \emph{fused} baseline, one of the $46$ cells falls
below $1$ (Section~\ref{sec:surface}).}
\label{fig:crossover}
\end{figure}

Gathering beats unfused dense in $0$ of $46$ measured cells and beats the
fused baseline in $1$ of $46$ --- at $d{=}128$, $B{=}4096$, $N{=}12$, ratio
$0.859$, where the fused kernel's fixed overhead dominates at small $N$. The
underlying numbers are tabulated in Appendix~\ref{app:surface}. These $46$
cells are a single sweep: GPU, \texttt{fp32}, eager mode. The CPU, \texttt{bf16},
compiled and CUDA-graph results are separate and smaller sweeps, and we do not
pool them into this count.

Across the surface the ratio stays within $1.16$--$2.46\times$. It is flat in
batch across a $128$-fold range, which suggests the outcome is not an artifact
of one operating point. It is \emph{not} flat in head width: at $N{=}512$ it
decreases monotonically as $d$ grows, and the sweep stops at $d{=}256$ with the
two most favourable remaining cells ($d \in \{128,256\}$ at $B{=}4096$,
$N{=}512$) skipped for memory. We therefore make no claim for $d > 256$, and we
regard the head-width trend as unresolved rather than as evidence either way.
Head widths above $256$ are also well outside the range used in multi-agent
communication, which is why the sweep was designed as it was; a reader
interested in the large-$d$ limit should treat this as an open question.

Figure~\ref{fig:decomp} and Table~\ref{tab:decomp} give two results, both
against the hypothesis they were meant to test. First, the aggregation saving
is negative: the sparse
$(B\!\cdot\!N,1,k)\times(B\!\cdot\!N,k,d)$ contraction is $3.4\times$ slower
than the dense $(B,N,N)\times(B,N,d)$ matmul it replaces at $N{=}512$, because
a batched matrix--vector product has far lower arithmetic intensity than a real
GEMM and cannot use the same hardware paths. Second, selection is the largest
single stage: \texttt{topk} alone costs $15.8\times$ the entire dense
aggregation at $N{=}512$, and $54\%$ of the gathered path's forward pass.

How much of this a better kernel could remove is a question the roofline model
can answer, and the answer is: a substantial part of the selection term, but
not the outcome. At $N{=}512$, $B{=}256$ the top-$k$ must read the
$268$\,MB score matrix and write values and indices, about $0.47$\,GB in total.
At the $1448.8$\,GB/s we measure as achievable that is a $0.32$\,ms floor
against the $3.897$\,ms observed, so PyTorch's sort-based top-$k$ is running at
roughly $8\%$ of achievable bandwidth on this shape. The gather stage, by
contrast, runs near bandwidth. Selection is therefore \emph{not} intrinsically
expensive in the way the raw decomposition suggests, and we withdraw any
suggestion that it is; the \texttt{sorted=False} measurement above recovers a
third of it with a one-word change. What survives is the ordering: substituting
a bandwidth-optimal selection kernel into the $N{=}512$ cell gives roughly
$3.5$\,ms for the gathered path against $1.99$\,ms unfused and $0.43$\,ms
fused. The conclusion does not depend on the selection kernel being bad. It
depends on the score matrix having to be formed and ranked at all, which is
assumption A2.

\subsection{The evaluated learned gate costs $\mathcal{O}(BN^3)$ memory}
\label{sec:memory}

Our per-agent learned gate over $k \in \{1,\dots,N-1\}$ broadcasts a
$(1,1,K,N)$ cumulative-keep tensor against $(B,N,K,1)$ gate probabilities with
$K = N-1$, materialising $(B,N,N-1,N)$ before reducing. Activation memory is
therefore $\mathcal{O}(BN^3)$ against dense attention's $\mathcal{O}(BN^2)$ and
the fused kernel's $\mathcal{O}(BNd)$.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{figures/fig5_memory.pdf}
\caption{Peak activation memory against agent count, batch $256$, with the
offset-corrected fitted exponent beside each arm. The evaluated gate
implementation grows cubically and is the first arm to exhaust the device;
gathering saves no memory over masking, because the gathered value tensor
replaces the zeros it avoids.}
\label{fig:memory}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:memory} validates the derivation. Fitting $\text{peak} \sim
N^p$ over $N \ge 48$ with a constant offset removed gives $p = 3.04$ for the
gate, against $1.96$ for dense, $2.02$ for masked, $1.98$ for gathered and
$1.36$ for fused. The absolute check is sharper: at $N{=}384$ the closed form
predicts $97.5\%$ of the measured peak. In practice the gate consumes
$55.2$\,GiB at $N{=}384$ (batch $256$) and exhausts the device at $N{=}512$;
analytically it exhausts a $96$\,GiB card at $N{=}465$, and at batch $4096$ at
$N{=}185$.

This is a result about the implementation we evaluated, not a lower bound on
learned gates. The same function admits an $\mathcal{O}(BN^2)$ formulation ---
a cumulative sum along $K$ rather than a broadcast against an explicit
cumulative-keep tensor --- and we report the cubic scaling as an easy and
costly implementation trap rather than as a property of adaptive selection.
It belongs with the pitfalls of Section~\ref{sec:pitfalls}, and its interest is
that the arm whose stated purpose is to reduce cost was the first to exhaust
the device.

\subsection{Cross-architecture check}
\label{sec:cpu}

Running the same arms on an x86\_64 CPU tests whether the ordering depends on
GPU kernel selection. It does not, but obtaining a usable measurement required
controlling an effect unrelated to attention. With the default glibc allocator
the dense, masked and gathered paths each allocate and free several multi-MiB
$(B,N,N)$ temporaries per forward, which the allocator returns to the kernel
and which are then re-faulted on the next call: at $N{=}192$ this adds
$\approx37{,}000$ minor page faults per forward and inflates latency by up to
$8.8\times$. The tax is proportional to the bytes of large temporaries each arm
churns, so it differs per arm and does not cancel in a ratio. In our archived
CPU run it landed hard enough on the dense arm at $N{=}48$ to invert the
ordering there --- the single cell in this study where a gathered path appeared
to beat a dense one on equal terms. It was an allocator measurement, not an
attention measurement.

With the heap pinned, the gathered path costs $2.0$--$4.8\times$ unfused dense
and $2.6$--$11.8\times$ the fused baseline, with both ratios increasing
monotonically in $N$ (ratios against unfused dense: $2.02$, $1.99$, $2.15$,
$3.68$, $4.56$, $4.82$ for $N = 6 \ldots 192$; an independent repeat under
different host load gives $1.95$, $1.88$, $2.22$, $3.45$, $4.91$, $5.00$). The
fused kernel is the only arm largely unaffected by the allocator, because CPU
flash-attention never materialises a $(B,N,N)$ tensor.

Two things are worth separating here. The gathered-versus-unfused penalty is of
the same order on both devices and, at $N{=}192$, is smaller on CPU
($\approx1.9$--$4.8\times$) than on GPU ($3.75\times$) --- agreement in sign,
not in magnitude. The much larger CPU figure against the \emph{fused} baseline
is dominated by a different effect: on CPU the fused kernel's tiling avoids
streaming a $37.7$\,MB score matrix through a $96$\,MiB L3 shared across two
sockets, giving it a $19.6\times$ advantage over unfused dense against
$1.38\times$ on the GPU. We report that as evidence about which dense baseline
matters, not as evidence about arithmetic intensity.

\subsection{Drift control}
\label{sec:drift}

Because clocks are unlocked and the host is shared, we re-ran the identical
sweep $\approx21$\,h later, twice: once on an idle second device of the same
model, and once on the original device under concurrent training load.

Table~\ref{tab:drift} gives the result. On an idle second device the ratios reproduce to within $3.2\%$ at every $N$,
so the measurement is a property of the algorithm and architecture rather than
of one physical card. Under concurrent load agreement degrades substantially,
concentrated at small and moderate $N$ where kernels are launch-bound; the
largest cells move by about $1\%$. The operational consequence is that headline
latency must be measured on an unloaded device, and that interleaving plus
minimum-over-samples reduces contention sensitivity without eliminating it.

Direction and magnitude should be reported separately. Under concurrent load
all eight ratio cells moved against the sparse path, by $+1.1\%$ to $+56.4\%$.
On the idle second device the movements are small and mixed: six of eight are
slightly in the sparse path's favour, none by more than $3.2\%$. The relevant
quantity is therefore the margin rather than the direction. The smallest
gathered/unfused-dense ratio we observe on GPU across every run, device, load,
precision and execution mode is $1.14\times$ (idle second device, $N{=}6$); the
smallest against the fused baseline is $0.92\times$, at $N{=}6$ and batch
$4096$, where fixed kernel overhead dominates. No condition we measured moves
any GPU cell within $14\%$ of a crossover against unfused dense, and the one
contention regime we could measure moves every cell further from one. We cannot
exclude an interference pattern that would penalise the dense path more than
the sparse one --- a competitor that evicted the dense path's working set
rather than adding launch latency might do so --- but none of the conditions
available to us does.
