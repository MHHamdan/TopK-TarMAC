\subsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

We time the communication module in isolation, with the training loop removed.
Several provisions below were forced by defects that produced spurious results
first (Section~\ref{sec:pitfalls}). Timing uses on-device CUDA events with an
explicit synchronisation on the correct device, and any diagnostic calling
\texttt{.item()} is disabled, because such a call synchronises the device
\emph{inside} the timed forward pass. Arms are timed interleaved repetition by
repetition rather than in sequential per-arm blocks, so that interference lands
on all arms at once and their ratios remain paired; the sequential design
produced swings above $100\%$ between identical configurations on this shared
host, which interleaving reduced to typically under $2\%$. We report $\min$ over
samples as the primary statistic, on the modelling assumption that interference
adds time rather than removing it; Appendix~\ref{app:dispersion} gives median,
IQR, inter-run spread and the sensitivity of every headline ratio to that
choice. GPU clocks could not be locked --- the account lacks the privilege.
Executed FLOPs are counted with \texttt{torch.utils.flop\_counter}, so latency
is always reported beside the arithmetic actually performed.

Unless stated otherwise: PyTorch 2.11 / CUDA 12.8, \texttt{fp32}, input
embedding width $64$, head width $d{=}16$, batch $256$, $k = 25\%$ of peers,
and an idle device.

Table~\ref{tab:headline} is the central measurement. The gathered
implementation does execute less arithmetic than unfused dense ---
$3553$ against $5167$ MFLOP at $N{=}512$ as the counter reports them, a $31\%$
reduction consistent with
Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} --- and it is nonetheless $3.28\times$ slower
than unfused dense and $15.18\times$ slower than the fused kernel. Correcting
the implementation helps without being sufficient: replacing the masked variant
with the gathered one improves the ratio against unfused dense from
$\approx1.40\times$ to $\approx1.16\times$ for $N \le 48$. The improvement does
not persist --- at $N{=}96$ and $N{=}192$ the gathered path is slower than the
masked one it corrects ($0.205$ vs $0.198$; $0.596$ vs $0.503$). The
explanation we offer for that reversal is the roofline account of
Section~\ref{sec:roofline} --- the gather's traffic grows faster than the
masked path's wasted arithmetic --- and it is an inference from the byte
accounting in Table~\ref{tab:roofline}, not a separate controlled experiment.

The two ends of this table are governed by different mechanisms, which bounds
how far the numbers generalise. For $N \le 96$ the unfused dense arm is nearly
flat at $0.12$--$0.13$\,ms while its work grows $256$-fold: those cells are
bound by kernel launch count rather than bandwidth or arithmetic, and the
$1.15$--$1.17\times$ ratios there reflect how many kernels each arm issues,
which makes them sensitive to how many arms share a timing group --- two of our
own sweeps disagree by $5\times$ on one such cell (Section~\ref{sec:pitfalls},
P7). From $N{=}192$ upward the working set exceeds L2, traffic is real, and the
roofline account of Section~\ref{sec:roofline} applies.

\paragraph{Robustness.} The ordering is unchanged under \texttt{torch.compile}
and CUDA-graph capture: at $N{=}512$ the ratio against unfused dense is
$3.29$, $3.30$ and $3.31$ for eager, compiled and graph-captured execution
respectively. It is also unchanged at batch $4096$, where the largest cells
become FLOP-bound. In \texttt{bf16} the gap is wider, reaching $19.7\times$ the
fused kernel at $N{=}512$. FlashAttention serves no arm here --- our fused
baseline passes a self-attention mask, which this build rejects for
FlashAttention in every mask form, and \texttt{fp32} excludes it again on dtype
--- so the fused arm runs PyTorch's memory-efficient CUTLASS kernel, read from
the profiler rather than asserted. The substitution runs against us: forcing
FlashAttention on the same unmasked \texttt{bf16} shape is $1.5$--$2.5\times$
faster than the kernel we do get, so a baseline able to reach it would widen
the gap we report. Appendix~\ref{app:verification} gives the probe and its
numbers.

\paragraph{Selection is tuned, and it matters how much.} The gathered path
calls \texttt{torch.topk} with \texttt{sorted=False}. The ordering of the $k$
retained entries is irrelevant here --- the softmax and the gather are permuted
consistently by the same indices, and we verify the two calls agree to
$5.4\times10^{-7}$ --- while the unsorted call was cheaper in every shape we
tested under PyTorch 2.11. The difference is not small on the stage that
dominates this path: with the default \texttt{sorted=True} the selection stage
costs up to $35\%$ more and the whole gathered arm $5$--$24\%$ more, which at
$N{=}192$ is the difference between a ratio of $2.90$ and one near $3.8$. We
report the tuned configuration throughout, because the question is whether a
tuned sparse implementation wins, not whether a careless one loses. The size of
that gap is itself part of the finding: a sparse path has more sites of
avoidable implementation overhead than a dense one, and an operation count sees
none of them.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Is there any $(d, B, N, k)$ where sparsity wins?}{Is there any (d, B, N, k) where sparsity wins?}}
\label{sec:surface}

Reporting isolated favourable cells as anomalies is not a characterisation. We
swept head width $d \in \{32,64,128,256\}$ against batch $\in \{32,256,4096\}$
and $N \in \{12,48,192,512\}$, taking for each cell the \emph{best} $k$ for the
gather path --- the most favourable operating point available to it.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{figures/fig2_crossover.pdf}

($2.40 \to 2.44 \to 2.10 \to 1.70$ at $B{=}256$: not strictly monotone, since

\label{fig:crossover}
\end{figure}

Gathering beats unfused dense in $0$ of $46$ measured cells and beats the
fused baseline in $1$ of $46$ --- at $d{=}128$, $B{=}4096$, $N{=}12$, ratio
$0.86$, where the fused kernel's fixed overhead dominates at small $N$. The
underlying numbers are tabulated in Appendix~\ref{app:surface}. These $46$
cells are a single sweep: GPU, \texttt{fp32}, eager mode. The CPU, \texttt{bf16},
compiled and CUDA-graph results are separate and smaller sweeps, and we do not
pool them into this count.

Across the surface the ratio stays within $1.17$--$2.44\times$. It is flat in
batch across a $128$-fold range, which suggests the outcome is not an artifact
of one operating point. It is \emph{not} flat in head width: at $N{=}512$ it
trends downward as $d$ grows ($2.19 \to 2.29 \to 1.81 \to 1.44$ at $B{=}32$),
though not strictly --- $d{=}64$ is slightly worse than $d{=}32$ at both batch
sizes we can read the trend from --- and the sweep stops at $d{=}256$ with the
two most favourable remaining cells skipped for memory. We therefore make no
claim for $d > 256$ and regard the head-width trend as unresolved. Head widths
above $256$ are outside the range used in the multi-agent communication systems
of Section~\ref{sec:related}, which is why the sweep was designed as it was.

\subsection{Where the time goes}
\label{sec:decomp}

Table~\ref{tab:headline} says \emph{that} the sparse path loses; the following
says where its time is spent. We time each stage in isolation under the same
protocol and form two quantities: the \emph{saving} the sparse contraction buys
($\texttt{agg\_dense} - \texttt{agg\_sparse}$) and the \emph{overhead}
selection costs
($\texttt{topk} + \texttt{gather} + \texttt{softmax}_k - \texttt{softmax}_N$).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig3_decomposition.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Left:} how the gathered path spends its time, as a share of
its full forward pass (absolute totals above each bar, in ms). Top-$k$
selection grows from $10\%$ to $51\%$ of the budget as $N$ rises, and selection
together with the gather and the softmax over $k$ grows from $29\%$ to $67\%$. \textbf{Right:} the
aggregation that sparsity was supposed to shrink. The sparse contraction is
slower than the dense matmul it replaces at every $N$, reaching $3.4\times$ at
$N{=}512$ while performing roughly four times fewer multiply--accumulates.}
\label{fig:decomp}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:decomp} and Table~\ref{tab:decomp} give two results, both
against the hypothesis they were meant to test. First, the aggregation saving
is negative: the sparse
$(B\!\cdot\!N,1,k)\times(B\!\cdot\!N,k,d)$ contraction is $3.4\times$ slower
than the dense $(B,N,N)\times(B,N,d)$ matmul it replaces at $N{=}512$, because
a batched matrix--vector product has far lower arithmetic intensity than a real
GEMM and cannot use the same hardware paths. Second, selection is the largest
single stage: \texttt{topk} alone costs $13.7\times$ the entire dense
aggregation at $N{=}512$, and $51\%$ of the gathered path's forward pass.

How much of this a better kernel could remove is a question the roofline model
answers: a substantial part of the selection term, but not the outcome. At
$N{=}512$, $B{=}256$ the top-$k$ must read the $268$\,MB score matrix and write
values and indices, about $0.47$\,GB; at the $1444$\,GB/s we measure as
achievable, moving those bytes and nothing else would take $0.32$\,ms against
the $3.426$\,ms observed, so PyTorch's top-$k$ runs at roughly $9\%$
\emph{copy-equivalent bandwidth efficiency} on this shape even unsorted. That
is a bandwidth-only lower bound and an optimistic one, since a real selection
kernel also compares, ranks and writes irregularly; the gather stage, by
contrast, already runs near bandwidth. Selection is therefore \emph{not}
intrinsically expensive in the way the raw decomposition suggests, and we
withdraw any suggestion that it is. What survives is the ordering: substituting
even that unattainable copy-equivalent select into the $N{=}512$ cell gives
roughly $3.4$\,ms against $1.99$\,ms unfused and $0.43$\,ms fused --- an
optimistic counterfactual that still loses, rather than a proof that no kernel
can cross over. The conclusion does not depend on the selection kernel being
bad. It depends on the score matrix having to be formed and ranked at all,
which is assumption A2.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The evaluated learned gate costs $\mathcal{O}(BN^3)$ memory}{The evaluated learned gate costs O(BN cubed) memory}}
\label{sec:memory}

Our per-agent learned gate over $k \in \{1,\dots,N-1\}$ broadcasts a
$(1,1,K,N)$ cumulative-keep tensor against $(B,N,K,1)$ gate probabilities with
$K = N-1$, materialising $(B,N,N-1,N)$ before reducing. Activation memory is
therefore $\mathcal{O}(BN^3)$ against dense attention's $\mathcal{O}(BN^2)$ and
the fused kernel's $\mathcal{O}(BNd)$.

Measurement confirms the derivation (Appendix~\ref{app:memory}). Fitting
$\text{peak} \sim N^p$ over $N \ge 48$ with a constant offset removed gives
$p = 3.04$ for the gate, against $1.96$ for dense, $2.02$ for masked, $1.98$ for
gathered and $1.36$ for fused, and at $N{=}384$ the closed form predicts
$97.5\%$ of the measured peak; the gate consumes $55.2$\,GiB at $N{=}384$,
batch $256$, and exhausts the device at $N{=}512$. This is a result about the
implementation we evaluated, not a lower bound on learned gates --- the same
function admits an $\mathcal{O}(BN^2)$ formulation --- so we report it as an
easy and costly implementation trap, belonging with the pitfalls of
Section~\ref{sec:pitfalls}. Its interest is that the arm whose stated purpose
is to reduce cost was the first to exhaust the device.

\subsection{Cross-architecture check}
\label{sec:cpu}

Running the same arms on an x86\_64 CPU tests whether the ordering depends on
GPU kernel selection. It does not, but obtaining a usable measurement required
controlling an effect unrelated to attention. With the default glibc allocator
the multi-MiB $(B,N,N)$ temporaries each arm allocates and frees per forward
are returned to the kernel and re-faulted on the next call: at $N{=}192$ this
adds $\approx37{,}000$ minor page faults per forward and inflates latency by up
to $8.8\times$. The tax is proportional to the bytes of large temporaries each
arm churns, so it differs per arm and does not cancel in a ratio, and in our
archived un-pinned run it landed hard enough on the dense arm at $N{=}48$ to
invert the ordering there. That was an allocator measurement, not an attention
measurement. We pin the heap and count minor faults per forward inside the
timed region; Appendix~\ref{app:cpu} gives the procedure and what the harness
records so the before-and-after can be inspected rather than taken on trust.

With the heap pinned, the gathered path is slower than both dense baselines at
every $N$: $1.96$--$3.13\times$ unfused dense and $2.54$--$28.9\times$ the
fused baseline. The $N{=}48$ inversion disappears entirely --- gathered is
$3.13\times$ slower there once the fault storm is removed, against an apparent
$0.81\times$ before --- and it was the only cell in this study, on either
device, where a gathered path appeared to beat a dense one on equal terms. The
fused kernel is the arm least affected by the allocator, because CPU
flash-attention never materialises a $(B,N,N)$ tensor.

Two effects are worth separating. The gathered-versus-unfused penalty is of the
same order on both devices and at $N{=}192$ is smaller on CPU ($2.10\times$)
than on GPU ($2.90\times$) --- agreement in sign, not magnitude. The much larger
CPU figure against the \emph{fused} baseline is dominated by a different
effect: the fused kernel's tiling avoids streaming a $37.7$\,MB score matrix
through a $96$\,MiB L3 shared across two sockets, giving it a $13.8\times$
advantage over unfused dense at $N{=}192$ against $4.6\times$ on the GPU. That
is evidence about which dense baseline matters, not about arithmetic
intensity.

\subsection{Drift control}
\label{sec:drift}

Because clocks are unlocked and the host is shared, we re-ran the identical
sweep twice on the same device: once with the device otherwise idle, and once
with a concurrent GEMM-and-allocation load running on it.

Table~\ref{tab:drift} in Appendix~\ref{app:drift} gives the result. With the
device idle the ratios reproduce to within $1.4\%$ at every $N$ and absolute
latency to within $2.6\%$. Under concurrent load agreement degrades sharply ---
absolute latency moves by a median of $68\%$ --- while the paired ratios move by
a median of $34\%$. The pairing buys less than that suggests: the \emph{worst}
ratio cell moves by $116\%$, marginally more than the worst absolute-latency
cell at $114\%$, so what pairing reliably removes is the typical drift, not the
tail. Headline latency must therefore be measured on an unloaded device, and
interleaving plus minimum-over-samples reduces contention sensitivity without
eliminating it.

Direction and magnitude should be reported separately. Under load all eight
ratio cells moved against the sparse path, by $+12\%$ to $+116\%$; on the idle
repeat the movements are small and mixed, none exceeding $1.4\%$. The relevant
quantity is therefore the margin. The smallest gathered/unfused-dense ratio we
observe on GPU across every run, precision and execution mode is $1.15\times$,
and on CPU $1.96\times$; the smallest against the fused baseline is
$0.89\times$, at $N{=}6$ and batch $4096$, where fixed kernel overhead
dominates. No condition we measured brings any GPU cell below $1.15\times$
unfused dense, and the one contention regime we could measure moves every cell
further from one. We cannot exclude an interference pattern that would penalise
the dense path more --- a competitor evicting its working set rather than adding
launch latency might --- but none available to us does. This is a same-device
repeat rather than a second card, because the second card was not free of other
tenants when these measurements were taken; it establishes stability across
runs and hours, not independence from one physical card.
