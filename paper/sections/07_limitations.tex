\paragraph{What the claim covers.} Every cost result here concerns
centralised-inference computational cost: the wall-clock latency and memory of
executing the communication module on a single device, where all agents'
observations are available to one process. It is not a claim about
bandwidth-constrained distributed deployment, where the objective is to reduce
messages physically transmitted and top-$k$ sparsification may well be correct
even though it does not reduce local compute. Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling}
says nothing about transmitted bytes.

\paragraph{Measurement envelope.} Single-round attention; scaled dot-product
attention with the fused backends available in PyTorch 2.11 / CUDA 12.8; input
embedding width $64$; head width $d \in \{16,32,64,128,256\}$; $N \le 512$;
batch $32$--$4096$; \texttt{fp32} and \texttt{bf16}; eager,
\texttt{torch.compile} and CUDA-graph execution; one GPU architecture (NVIDIA
Blackwell, sm\_120) and one x86\_64 CPU. We make no claim outside it. Within it
the head-width trend is unresolved: at $N{=}512$ the ratio trends downward in
$d$ without being strictly monotone, and the sweep ends at $d{=}256$, so we do
not know where, or whether, it reaches $1$ for larger heads.

\paragraph{Our sparse implementation is not optimal, and we can bound by how
much.} \Gath{} is a straightforward PyTorch implementation using
\texttt{gather} and a batched contraction, not a hand-written fused kernel. We
removed one avoidable cost --- \texttt{torch.topk} sorts by default, worth
$5$--$24\%$ of the arm --- and one remains: PyTorch's top-$k$ still runs at
roughly $9\%$ copy-equivalent bandwidth efficiency, so a selection-optimal
kernel would recover a substantial part of the remaining selection term.
Substituting even a copy-equivalent select --- optimistic, since a real
selection kernel must also compare and rank --- still leaves the gathered path
near $3.4$\,ms at $N{=}512$ against $1.99$\,ms unfused and $0.43$\,ms fused, so
the ordering survives; but a reader should treat our ratios as upper bounds on
the penalty, and a fully fused selection--gather--contraction kernel remains
the most informative single follow-up. It could reduce intermediate traffic,
launch overhead and the cost of ranking; it could not avoid forming the score
matrix, which is assumption A2. We do not claim that no implementation can ever
cross over --- only that none available to us does, and that the analytic
ceiling bounds what any of them could win.

\paragraph{Generality across architectures.} The intensity argument of
Section~\ref{sec:roofline} predicts the same qualitative disadvantage wherever
the gathered path stays below the relevant machine balance, but that argument
is device-independent by construction and so cannot itself be evidence of
device-independence. Our empirical support is one GPU architecture and one CPU
on which the ordering holds with ratios of the same order, plus a same-device
drift repeat. We were unable to replicate on a different GPU generation --- the
older hardware that produced this project's earlier runs is no longer available
--- so a reader who weights architectural replication heavily should discount
the generality accordingly. The CPU comparison against the \emph{fused}
baseline is also dominated by cache blocking rather than arithmetic intensity,
making it weaker evidence for the intensity mechanism than the unfused
comparison is.

\paragraph{Measurement effects, controlled and uncontrolled.} Our CPU numbers
required pinning the glibc heap; without it, page faults on recycled $(B,N,N)$
temporaries inflate arms unequally and can invert an ordering
(Section~\ref{sec:cpu}). Our GPU ridge point reproduces to within $2\%$, but
the CPU one is a cache-bandwidth figure that does not: repeat measurements give
$1.35$ and $3.68$\,FLOP/byte and a DRAM-resident probe lower still, so we use
it only where the conclusion holds across that whole range. What remains
uncontrolled is that small-$N$ cells are launch-bound and their ratios depend
on how many arms share a timing group --- two of our own sweeps disagree by
$5\times$ on one such cell --- so ratios at $N \le 96$ should be read with that
in mind.

\paragraph{Fused baseline and unlocked clocks.} Our fused arm passes a
self-attention mask, which this build rejects for FlashAttention in any form,
so all fused numbers are PyTorch's memory-efficient CUTLASS kernel, read from
the profiler rather than inferred; forcing FlashAttention on the same unmasked
shape is $1.5$--$2.5\times$ faster, so a baseline able to reach it would widen
the gap we report. We also lacked the privilege to lock GPU clocks, and
compensate with interleaved timing, minimum-over-samples reporting, published
dispersion (Appendix~\ref{app:dispersion}) and an explicit drift control
(Section~\ref{sec:drift}). Absolute latency under concurrent load can move by
more than $100\%$; median ratio drift is roughly half the median
absolute-latency drift, though the worst loaded ratio cell moves as far as the
worst latency cell, so stating claims in ratios buys stability in the typical
case rather than in the tail. The drift control is a same-device repeat, so it
establishes stability across runs and hours, not independence from one physical
card.

\paragraph{Statistical power of the training study.} Five seeds per arm gives a
minimum attainable two-sided permutation $p$ of $0.0079$ against a first Holm
threshold of $0.05/6 = 0.0083$, so any Holm rejection requires the smallest
family $p$-value to sit at the discrete permutation floor
(Section~\ref{sec:training}).
Resolving the $d \approx 0.6$ effect suggested by C1 would need roughly $45$
seeds per arm at two-sided $\alpha = 0.05$ and $80\%$ power, and roughly $70$
at the family-corrected $\alpha = 0.05/6$; both figures come from the standard
two-sample normal-approximation formula
$n = 2\bigl(z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta}\bigr)^2/d^2$, which is a guide to order
of magnitude rather than a power calculation for the exact permutation test we
actually run. We should be direct that this was a
design choice rather than a hard constraint: the entire $60$-run study consumed
$31.9$ GPU-hours against a $200$-hour guardrail, and a properly powered C1
would have cost about $37.8$. We allocated the budget to the measurement
campaign instead, which we would do again given that the computational result
is the paper's contribution --- but the six nulls are a consequence of that
allocation, not of an unaffordable experiment, and a reader is entitled to
weigh them accordingly.

\paragraph{Environment.} The partially observable \texttt{simple\_spread}
variant is our own, documented in Section~\ref{sec:training} and released. It
carries a difficulty confound across $N$ that we measure and publish, which is
why no across-$N$ return comparison appears here; its zeroed visibility mask
does not distinguish an absent peer from one at the origin, affecting
$\le 0.15\%$ of visible peers; and at $N{=}12$ neither trained arm beats a
uniform-random policy, so no contrast at that agent count is interpretable.
Results on MPE tasks may not transfer to environments with different
observation structure, and our positive controls establish that the
communicating architecture helps rather than isolating the channel, since the
communicating arms carry $7$--$19\%$ more parameters.
