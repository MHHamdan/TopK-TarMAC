\subsection{Setting and three implementations of one function}
\label{sec:three}

Consider one round of scaled dot-product attention over $N$ agents, per batch
element, with head width $d$. Agent $i$ forms a query $q_i$, and every agent
contributes a key and a value; self-attention is masked. Write
$s_i = q_i K^\top \in \mathbb{R}^N$ for the score vector and
$a_i = \mathrm{softmax}(s_i)$ for the attention weights. Dense attention
computes $m_i = a_i V$.

A top-$k$ variant keeps only the $k$ largest entries of $a_i$ and renormalises.
We call a mechanism of this kind \emph{score-then-select}: for each query it
first forms the complete score vector $s_i \in \mathbb{R}^N$ and only then
selects the $k$ largest entries. The term is load-bearing throughout. Every
analytic claim below is a claim about that class, not about content-dependent
selection in general, and Section~\ref{sec:bound} draws the boundary
explicitly. The same function admits implementations with very different costs,
and the distinction between them is the one this paper turns on.

\begin{description}
  \item[Masked top-$k$.] Compute $a_i$ in full, multiply by a $\{0,1\}$ mask,
    renormalise, and evaluate $m_i = \tilde{a}_i V$ with $\tilde{a}_i$ still
    dense in memory. The discarded entries are materialised as zeros, so the
    executed dense aggregation MAC count is unchanged from dense; selection,
    masking and renormalisation are added on top of it.
  \item[Gathered top-$k$.] Take the top-$k$ of the raw scores, softmax over
    those $k$ values only, gather the corresponding value rows into a
    $(B,N,k,d)$ tensor, and contract
    $(B\!\cdot\!N,1,k)\times(B\!\cdot\!N,k,d)$. No zeros are materialised and
    the aggregation genuinely shrinks.
  \item[Fused dense.] Compute the same function as dense through a fused kernel
    \citep{dao2022flashattention,rabe2021selfattention} that never
    materialises the $(B,N,N)$ weight matrix.
\end{description}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig1_schematic.pdf}
\caption{The same top-$k$ attention, computed three ways. All three evaluate
every pairwise query--key interaction, because a score-then-select rule cannot
rank what it has not scored. They
differ in what happens next: the fused kernel evaluates them tilewise and never
materialises the full $N \times N$ weight matrix;
the masked variant stores the discarded entries as zeros and multiplies them
anyway, so its executed aggregation MAC count equals dense's; only the gathered
variant compacts the kept rows and shrinks the product. The efficiency argument
examined in this paper is an argument about the third row.}
\label{fig:schematic}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:schematic} sets the three side by side. Gathered and masked
top-$k$ compute the same function: softmax is monotone within a row, so the
top-$k$ weights are the top-$k$ scores, and a softmax over just those $k$
scores equals the dense softmax restricted to them and renormalised --- verified
to $<10^{-10}$ in \texttt{float64} and pinned by a regression test
(Appendix~\ref{app:verification}). The operation-count argument is about the
gathered variant; timing the masked variant measures an implementation
artifact, and timing either against unfused dense alone ignores what a
production kernel costs. We report all three throughout, each against both
dense baselines.

\subsection{A ceiling on what sparsity can save}
\label{sec:bound}

Analytic counts here are multiply--accumulates (MACs). Under the convention
that a multiply--add is two FLOPs every term below doubles and every ratio is
unchanged, so the ceiling is the same number either way; we say MACs where the
count is analytic and FLOPs only where a counter attached to the running
program produced the figure. Counting the two matrix products that constitute
attention, and excluding the shared input and output projections:
\begin{equation}
C_{\text{scores}} = N^2 d, \quad C_{\text{agg}} = N^2 d, \quad
C_{\text{dense}} = 2N^2 d, \qquad
C_{\text{top-}k} = \underbrace{N^2 d}_{\text{all scores}} + \underbrace{Nkd}_{\text{aggregate }k}.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Score-then-select ceiling]
\label{prop:ceiling}
Let the retained set be fixed by a score-then-select rule --- one whose kept
indices are determined only after the complete score vector of each query has
been formed. Then
\begin{equation}
\frac{C_{\text{dense}}}{C_{\text{top-}k}}
= \frac{2N^2 d}{N^2 d + Nkd} = \frac{2N}{N+k} < 2
\qquad \text{for all } k \ge 1,
\end{equation}
with supremum $2$ approached only as $k/N \to 0$. Within this class, no choice
of $k$ or $N$ yields more than a $2\times$ reduction in attention MACs.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$C_{\text{top-}k} > N^2 d = \tfrac12 C_{\text{dense}}$ for every $k \ge 1$
since $Nkd > 0$. The ratio decreases monotonically in $k$ and tends to $2$ as
$k/N \to 0$.
\end{proof}

We use $N^2d$ rather than $N(N{-}1)d$ because it is the count the
implementations execute: self-masking writes $-\infty$ into the diagonal of a
full $N \times N$ score matrix rather than skipping work. Counting the diagonal
exactly would give $2(N{-}1)/\bigl((N{-}1)+k\bigr)$, strictly smaller than
$2N/(N+k)$ for every $1 \le k \le N-1$, so Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} is a
valid upper bound under either convention and slightly loose under the exact
count. The figure $1 - k/(N-1)$ describes the value-aggregation term alone; read
as \emph{the} saving it overstates the achievable reduction by a factor
approaching two, before any question of whether it is realised in time.

The assumptions are worth separating, because only one of them is
load-bearing. \textbf{(A1) Single round}: with $R$ rounds both terms scale by
$R$ and the ratio is unchanged. \textbf{(A2) Full-score selection}: the
retained set is determined only after the complete candidate score vector has
been computed, so all $N$ scores must be formed.
\textbf{(A3) Per head}: with $H$ heads of width $d/H$ both terms scale
identically. \textbf{(A4) Projections excluded}: including the shared $qkv$ and
output projections moves the ratio strictly closer to $1$, so the bound
remains valid. \textbf{(A5) Selection cost excluded}: counting the top-$k$
operation itself can only decrease the ratio, and Section~\ref{sec:decomp}
shows this term is large in practice.

A2 is the assumption that carries the result, and it is worth being exact about
what it does and does not cover, because the proposition is only as useful as
its domain is precise. A2 is a statement about \emph{when} the retained set is
fixed --- after the full score vector exists --- and not about whether the rule
depends on the data. Mechanisms that are content-dependent but not
score-then-select are outside the class and are not constrained by
Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling}: exact or approximate nearest-neighbour
retrieval, clustering and routing, indexing, and hashing can all identify
candidates without materialising the complete pairwise score matrix. Routing
Transformer's online $k$-means routing \citep{roy2021routing} and Reformer's
locality-sensitive hash \citep{kitaev2020reformer} are content-dependent in
every ordinary sense and still evaluate scores only for candidate pairs; a
hierarchical rule that ranks compressed blocks and then scores only the
selected ones \citep{yuan2025nsa} is likewise outside A2. What such rules pay
instead is a selector cost, and Proposition~\ref{prop:escape} is what charges
them for it. When A2 fails, the ceiling lifts.

\begin{proposition}[Escape]
\label{prop:escape}
Suppose the kept set is determined \emph{without} forming the complete score
vector --- structurally, or by a retrieval, routing, clustering, indexing or
hashing rule --- at total selector cost $C_{\mathrm{sel}}(N,k,d)$, and that
attention scores are then evaluated only for the $Nk$ retained pairs. (We keep
the subscript $\mathrm{struct}$ below for the structural case that motivates
it; the algebra is the same for any pre-score selector.) Then
\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{struct}} = 2Nkd + C_{\mathrm{sel}},
\qquad
\frac{C_{\text{dense}}}{C_{\mathrm{struct}}}
  = \frac{2N^2 d}{2Nkd + C_{\mathrm{sel}}}.
\end{equation}
\emph{(i)} If $C_{\mathrm{sel}} = o(Nkd)$ --- in particular for a fixed or
precomputed pattern, where $C_{\mathrm{sel}} \approx 0$ --- the ratio is
\emph{asymptotic} to $N/k$, that is
$\bigl(C_{\text{dense}}/C_{\mathrm{struct}}\bigr)\big/(N/k) \to 1$. Exact
equality with $N/k$ holds only when $C_{\mathrm{sel}} = 0$; for every
$C_{\mathrm{sel}} > 0$ the ratio is strictly smaller. \emph{(ii)} If only
$C_{\mathrm{sel}} = o(N^2 d)$, the ratio is no longer bounded by $2$: with
$k/N \to 0$ it diverges, though not in general at the rate $N/k$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Both statements read off the displayed ratio. For (i), $2Nkd + C_{\mathrm{sel}}
= 2Nkd\,(1+o(1))$, so
$C_{\text{dense}}/C_{\mathrm{struct}} = (N/k)\,(1+o(1))^{-1}$, whose quotient
with $N/k$ tends to $1$; substituting $C_{\mathrm{sel}} = 0$ gives
$2N^2 d/2Nkd = N/k$ exactly, and any $C_{\mathrm{sel}} > 0$ strictly enlarges
the denominator, so the ratio is then strictly below $N/k$. For (ii), if
$k/N \to 0$ then $2Nkd = o(N^2 d)$, so the whole denominator is $o(N^2 d)$ and
the ratio diverges.
\end{proof}

Qualifying rules include a fixed communication topology, a spatial or distance
prior, a learned \emph{static} graph, a locality-sensitive hash, and
clustering- or retrieval-based routing. The selector-cost term is not
decoration: a fixed topology or a precomputed spatial prior really is free at
inference and lands in case~(i), whereas a hash must be evaluated and its
buckets assembled \citep{kitaev2020reformer} and a routing rule must maintain
and query its clusters \citep{roy2021routing}, so their $C_{\mathrm{sel}}$ has
to be counted rather than assumed away. Case~(i) is why Longformer and BigBird
obtain asymptotic savings where score-then-select top-$k$ cannot
\citep{beltagy2020longformer,zaheer2020bigbird}: their patterns are fixed
before any score is formed, so nothing is spent deciding. Either case is a
concrete design target for a successor method --- to exceed $2\times$, a method
must decide where to look before looking, and spend less doing so than it
saves. In an environment with spatial structure a distance-calibrated selector
is the natural candidate.

Proposition~\ref{prop:escape} concerns operation counts only. Selection that
does not require the complete score vector removes the fixed $<2\times$
ceiling; when $C_{\mathrm{sel}} = o(Nkd)$ the achievable count ratio is
asymptotic to $N/k$, and exact $N/k$ is recovered only when the selector is
free. It does not follow that any particular such method attains a
proportional \emph{speedup}, which is a separate question and one the rest of
this paper suggests should be asked separately.

\subsection{Why the ceiling is not the binding constraint}
\label{sec:roofline}

Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} bounds a quantity that, in this regime, does not
govern runtime. For memory-bound kernels time tracks bytes moved rather than
arithmetic performed \citep{williams2009roofline}, and the two paths differ far
more in bytes than in FLOPs.

We compute arithmetic intensity $\mathrm{AI} = \text{FLOPs} / \text{bytes}$
analytically per stage, counting each tensor read and written once, and compare
it against a \emph{measured} machine balance: a large GEMM gives achievable
FLOP/s, a large device-to-device copy gives achievable bandwidth, and their
ratio is the ridge point above which a kernel is compute-bound. Two caveats
bound its use. Counting each tensor once ignores cache residency, so at small
$N$ --- where the working set fits in the GPU's $128$\,MB L2 --- the analytic
byte counts overstate traffic and the model does not apply. And our CPU
bandwidth probe uses buffers smaller than the host's $96$\,MiB L3, so the CPU
ridge is a cache-bandwidth figure that does not reproduce to better than a
factor of a few: repeat measurements give $1.35$ and $3.68$\,FLOP/byte, and a
DRAM-resident probe lower still. We therefore treat it as a range and use it
only where the conclusion holds across the whole range. Two such statements
carry the CPU argument: the gathered path's intensity of $\approx0.66$ lies
below every estimate, and the dense path's saturating intensity of
$(4d{+}5)/16 = 4.31$ above every estimate. The finite-$N$ dense intensities in
between are not stable in that sense, and we do not lean on them.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig4_roofline.pdf}
\caption{Roofline with measured machine balance. Marker heights are the roof

On the CPU, whose balance is more than an order of magnitude lower and is

\label{fig:roofline}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:roofline} gives the result, and Table~\ref{tab:roofline} in
Appendix~\ref{app:roofline} the numbers behind it. The
gathered path adds two stages that move substantial memory for no arithmetic
--- the top-$k$ selection and the gather --- so its intensity is pinned near
$0.66$ and does not improve with $N$. Dense intensity, by contrast, rises towards $(4d{+}5)/16 = 4.31$. At $N{=}512$ the sparse path moves $4.16$\,GB to perform
$2.76$\,GFLOP, against dense's $1.11$\,GB for $4.63$\,GFLOP: $3.8\times$ the
traffic for $0.6\times$ the arithmetic.

Where the model's assumptions hold it predicts well. At $N \in \{192, 512\}$,
where the working set exceeds L2 and traffic is real, both paths attain a
substantial fraction of achievable bandwidth and the ratio of analytic bytes
tracks the ratio of measured latencies. At $N \le 48$ it does not
apply, and Section~\ref{sec:threecurves} shows those cells are governed by
kernel launch count instead.

The quantity this analysis bounds is a ratio of intensities --- roughly $0.16$
at large $N$ --- a property of the two algorithms rather than of any device
constant, so the qualitative disadvantage should be expected wherever the
gathered path stays below the relevant machine balance. That is not itself
evidence that it does: an argument constructed to be device-independent cannot
demonstrate device-independence. Section~\ref{sec:cpu} supplies a second
architecture, and Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states what it establishes.
