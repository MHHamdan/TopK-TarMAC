We set out to make inter-agent attention cheaper by restricting each agent to
its top-$k$ peers, and the efficiency case for doing so rested on an operation
count. Examining that count, and then measuring what runs, produced three
separate negative results at three levels of abstraction.

The count is smaller than it appears: because a score-then-select rule must
form every score before it can rank them, only the value-aggregation product is
sparsifiable --- one of the two equal multiply--accumulate terms --- and the
achievable reduction is bounded by $2\times$ rather than by
$1-k/(N-1)$. The count need not be executed: masking the dense attention
matrix leaves the dense aggregation matmul in place, so an implementation
written that way executes the same dense aggregation MACs as dense attention,
plus selection and masking overhead, while a FLOP model reports a large saving.
And the executed saving need not be time: a genuinely sparse gather, which
we verified computes the same function while executing fewer operations, was
slower than unfused dense in all $46$ cells of our GPU sweep and slower than a
fused kernel in $45$ of them. Selection and gather move memory for no
arithmetic, the sparse contraction is slower than the dense one it replaces,
and at $N{=}512$ the sparse path moves $3.8\times$ the traffic for $0.6\times$
the arithmetic.

What does not follow is as important as what does. This is not evidence that
sparse multi-agent communication is a bad idea. The result is confined to
centralised inference on a single device; in bandwidth-constrained distributed
settings the objective is the number of messages transmitted, which we neither
analyse nor measure. Nor have we shown that no implementation can cross over:
we removed one slack in ours --- an unnecessary sort, worth $5$--$24\%$ --- and
quantified the one that remains, a selection kernel running at roughly $9\%$
copy-equivalent bandwidth efficiency whose bandwidth-only counterfactual
narrows the margin without reversing it. What survives every correction we
could make is the requirement to form and rank the full score matrix, which is
the assumption the ceiling rests on.

The constructive half of the result is Proposition~\ref{prop:escape}. A method
that fixes what it will attend to \emph{without} forming the complete score
vector --- by topology, by a spatial prior, by a hash, by clustering or
retrieval --- is not subject to the ceiling, and its count ratio is asymptotic
to $N/k$ when the cost of running its selector is $o(Nkd)$; exact $N/k$ is
recovered only when that selector is free. That condition is satisfied outright
by a fixed or precomputed pattern and has to be checked for anything learned,
hashed or routed; it is the term a successor method should budget for first.
This is not a hypothetical class: it is what the structural
selectors in the multi-agent literature already do, and what makes the
asymptotic savings in Longformer and BigBird real. In an environment with
spatial structure, distance-calibrated routing is the natural instance. A fused
kernel that eliminates the intermediate traffic between selection, gather and
contraction is the other direction worth pursuing, and would settle how much of
our measured penalty is implementation and how much is inherent.

The methodological lesson is not specific to multi-agent RL. An operation count
is a model of cost, and like any model it can fail in two ways: it can
mis-estimate the quantity it describes, and that quantity can fail to govern
the outcome anyone cares about. Both happened here, with a third failure --- the
implementation not executing the counted algorithm --- between them. The remedy
is cheap: report executed operation counts beside measured latency, name the
baseline, and state the envelope. We release the harness, the corrected
implementation, the pre-registration and the seven benchmarking pitfalls
(Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).
