\subsection{Attention-based inter-agent communication}

CommNet \citep{sukhbaatar2016commnet} broadcasts mean-pooled hidden states;
TarMAC \citep{das2019tarmac} introduces signature-based targeting so that
receivers weight senders; IC3Net \citep{singh2018ic3net} gates whether an agent
speaks at all; ATOC \citep{jiang2018atoc} forms bounded communication groups;
DGN \citep{jiang2018dgn} restricts attention to a spatial neighbourhood; MAT
\citep{wen2022mat} casts the joint policy as a sequence model. The pattern
persists in applied systems: MARL-IoTP \citep{hamdan2026marliotp} pairs
heterogeneous MAPPO agents with an attention-based message aggregator for edge
orchestration, and ablates that aggregator against mean pooling. Several of
these works motivate restricted communication with a cost argument. DGN, for
instance, observes that
receiving a large amount of information ``requires high bandwidth and incurs
high computational complexity'' \citep{jiang2018dgn} --- a claim about both
transmitted bytes and local compute, of which only the second is what this
paper measures.

Two points about this group matter for what follows. First, the restriction
mechanisms are mostly \emph{structural}: DGN's neighbourhood is a fixed spatial
prior, so it falls squarely under Proposition~\ref{prop:escape}. ATOC is the
more nuanced case --- its groups are formed by a learned attention unit that
decides \emph{whether} an agent should initiate communication, so it is not a
fixed pattern, but the grouping is settled before the communication attention
weights are computed, so its cost enters as the selector term of
Proposition~\ref{prop:escape} rather than as a full score matrix. Neither
mechanism is the score-then-select rule that
Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} constrains.

Second, we did not find a method in
the papers we audit
that selects peers by attention \emph{score} after computing all
scores, which is the mechanism this paper analyses. We flag that explicitly
because it determines what our result does and does not bear on: we are
evaluating a design we proposed and built, and the ceiling of
Section~\ref{sec:bound} constrains that design, not the structural selectors
above.

One proposal outside that audited set is the closest published relative of the
mechanism we examine: the adaptive sparse communication graph of
\citet{sun2020sparsecomm}, which learns which peers to attend to by replacing
softmax with a generalisation of sparsemax, so that the normalisation itself
emits exact zeros. It motivates that design partly through scalability --- the
paper states that the resulting sparsity ``can reduce the complexity of both
the forward pass and the back-propagation of the policy and value networks'' ---
and it explicitly considers, and rejects, hard top-$k$ thresholding as
non-differentiable. Two things follow. First, the design we set out to build is
a natural one to reach for here. Second, because the sparsity-inducing map is
applied to raw attention logits already formed for every agent pair, the
selection is \emph{post-score}: under our accounting it removes aggregation
work but not the score-computation term, so it sits inside the class
Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} constrains. That is a statement about where the
method lands in our accounting, not a contradiction of its claims --- its
reported gains are in sample efficiency at up to $30$ agents, it reports no
measured runtime, and the systems question we ask is not one it asks.

\subsection{Efficient and sparse attention}

The efficient-attention literature is where the operation-count argument for
sparsity is most developed, and it separates into mechanisms our two
propositions treat differently. \emph{Predetermined patterns}: Longformer
\citep{beltagy2020longformer} and BigBird \citep{zaheer2020bigbird} fix which
pairs interact in advance --- sliding windows, global tokens, random edges ---
so the score computation itself becomes sparse and the saving is asymptotic;
their selector is essentially free, which is case~(i) of
Proposition~\ref{prop:escape}. \emph{Routed or hashed candidate sets}: Reformer
\citep{kitaev2020reformer} restricts candidate interactions by a
locality-sensitive hash, and Routing Transformer \citep{roy2021routing} by
online $k$-means clustering of queries and keys. Both are content-dependent,
both evaluate scores only for candidate pairs, and together they are why
Proposition~\ref{prop:escape} carries a selector term at all: hashing,
clustering and bucketing are cheaper than the score matrix but they are not
free. \emph{Kernelised attention}: Performer
\citep{choromanski2021performers} does not select a subset of interactions in
this sense at all --- it avoids materialising the quadratic score matrix
through a kernel factorisation that approximates the softmax attention map.
The first two groups escape Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} because they decide
what to keep without forming the complete score matrix; Performer falls outside
the proposition's premise, since it keeps every interaction and reorders the
algebra instead.

Top-$k$ attention in the sequence setting is the closest external analogue of
the mechanism we build, and it makes a distinction we rely on.
\citet{gupta2021topk} process queries in chunks and, for each query, compute
its $k$ largest scores against all keys \emph{exactly} --- a score-then-select
rule in our sense. Their stated benefit is memory: chunking reduces peak
attention memory from $\Omega(L_Q L_K)$ to $O(C L_K)$ for chunk size $C$ while
remaining a drop-in replacement for vanilla attention in pretrained models
without corrective pre-training, and they describe chunk size as trading
maximum memory against the slowdown from processing chunks sequentially. Memory
efficiency, operation-count efficiency and wall-clock efficiency are three
different quantities, and a mechanism can buy one while paying in another. Ours
is a question about the second and the third, in the substantially smaller
agent-count regime of multi-agent communication.

Sparsemax \citep{martins2016sparsemax} and $\alpha$-entmax
\citep{peters2019entmax} obtain exact zeros from the normalisation rather than
a hard cutoff --- the family \citet{sun2020sparsecomm} generalises for
multi-agent communication. Those papers make no claim about attention cost; the
observation that their zeros arrive \emph{after} the scores exist, and that
they are therefore subject to our ceiling, is our inference rather than theirs.
Gumbel-softmax \citep{jang2017gumbel} provides the
relaxation used by learned discrete gates. Constrained-RL formulations
\citep{achiam2017cpo,stooke2020pidlagrangian} supply general machinery for
optimising under a constraint, which we adapt to a communication budget rather
than trading the budget against return at a
fixed weight. We implement entmax and a Lagrangian budget as arms in
Section~\ref{sec:training}.

\subsection{Sparse routing and mixture-of-experts}

Score-then-select top-$k$ is used at scale in sparse mixture-of-experts,
where a router scores every expert and dispatches each token to the best $k$
\citep{shazeer2017moe,fedus2022switch}. The structure of the argument is the
same one we examine, and so is the exemption: these architectures are designed
so that the router is small relative to the expert computation it gates, so the
analogue of our $N^2 d$ score term is a minor cost rather than an equal one,
and the saving is real. That contrast is instructive. Whether
score-then-select pays depends on the ratio between the cost of scoring all
candidates and the cost of the work being skipped --- large in MoE, and equal
to one in single-head attention, which is exactly Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling}.

\subsection{Measuring efficiency rather than counting it}

That operation counts, memory traffic, kernel fusion and realised latency are
distinct quantities is established in the systems literature and in parts of
the attention literature. The roofline model \citep{williams2009roofline}
formalises when arithmetic ceases to govern runtime. FlashAttention
\citep{dao2022flashattention} and memory-efficient attention
\citep{rabe2021selfattention} make the point from the other direction: the
winning implementations of dense attention reduce memory traffic without
changing the operation count at all, which is precisely the axis a FLOPs-based
argument cannot see.

One precedent anticipates the distinction this paper turns on. Longformer
reports runtime and memory for \emph{three} implementations of the same
attention pattern --- a loop version, a vectorised ``chunks'' version and a
custom CUDA kernel --- and notes that the chunked variant consumes roughly
twice the optimal memory because it computes some zero values. In our terms,
which its authors do not use, that is an over-computing vectorised
implementation measured separately from a genuinely sparse one, with the
difference reported.

\paragraph{A reporting audit.} Motivated by that precedent, we checked whether
efficiency claims in the papers we drew on are accompanied by a measurement.
For each paper we read the full text, including appendices and the official
supplement where one exists, searched it for seven temporal keywords, and
recorded whether it makes a computational-efficiency or complexity claim about
its mechanism, whether it reports any measured temporal quantity attributable
to that mechanism, and the figure, table or sentence that settled the decision.
Appendix~\ref{app:audit} states the protocol and its one scope caveat, and the
completed sheet is released, because a ``no'' is an absence claim and an
absence claim is only as good as the search behind it.
Table~\ref{tab:audit} reports the result.

Among the five multi-agent papers making an efficiency claim, one reports a
measured temporal quantity for its mechanism; among the six
efficient-attention and mixture-of-experts papers, five do. We draw no
conclusion about either field from twelve papers. The asymmetry is worth
stating only because it describes the evidence base we ourselves worked from
when we adopted the operation-count argument, and because the argument does not
transfer cleanly in any case: the efficient-attention results are obtained at
sequence lengths of $10^3$--$10^5$, while
multi-agent communication operates at $N \lesssim 10^2$, so the hardware
bottleneck the two regimes face need not be the same one --- which is precisely
what Section~\ref{sec:measurement} measures.

\paragraph{Where sparse attention does pay.} Recent systems work supplies the
other side of that ledger, and we state it plainly because it bounds our
conclusion. Native Sparse Attention \citep{yuan2025nsa} ranks compressed
key--value blocks and attends within only the selected blocks, with kernels
written for the resulting access pattern; it reports up to $9.0\times$ forward,
$6.0\times$ backward and $11.6\times$ decoding speedups over full attention at
$64$k context. MiniMax Sparse Attention \citep{lai2026msa} co-designs blockwise
selection with its GPU execution path, including an exp-free top-$k$ selection
kernel, and reports $14.2\times$ prefill and $7.6\times$ decoding wall-clock
speedups at $1$M context on H800. We take these at face value; they sharpen our
scope rather than contradict it. Whether sparsity produces wall-clock savings
depends on the ratio between the work avoided and the arithmetic and memory
cost of selecting and executing the sparse pattern. At $10^5$--$10^6$ tokens the
skipped computation is enormous, selection is amortised over blocks rather than
performed per pair, and the sparse path is kernel-co-designed. At
$N \lesssim 10^2$ none of those three holds: the avoided work is small,
per-pair selection dominates, and the sparse path is composed from
general-purpose operators. Our claim is about that regime, and what we identify
is what a successor design has to change --- which is, in effect, what these
systems changed.

\paragraph{Empirical rigour in MARL.} Our training protocol follows the
recommendations of \citet{papoudakis2021epymarl} for systematic MARL
benchmarking. Reporting effect sizes and intervals rather than bare means is a
rule we add on top of those recommendations. Our environments come
from the PettingZoo/MPE family \citep{terry2020pettingzoo}, and our learner is
MAPPO \citep{yu2022mappo}.
