Each of the following produced a misleading measurement during this study
before it was
caught. We report them as reusable guidance for anyone benchmarking attention
variants, because a benchmarking result is only as trustworthy as the harness
that produced it. In every case the symptom was a plausible result rather than
an obvious error; Table~\ref{tab:pitfalls} summarises them.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\small
\caption{Seven ways our harness measured something other than the algorithm.}
\label{tab:pitfalls}
\begin{tabular}{p{0.29\linewidth}p{0.33\linewidth}p{0.30\linewidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Pitfall} & \textbf{Symptom before diagnosis} & \textbf{Corrective practice} \\
\midrule
P1. A diagnostic \texttt{.item()} inside the timed region &
Dense latency nearly flat in $N$, read as evidence that the
$\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ cost does not manifest &
Gate diagnostics behind a flag that is off while timing \\
\addlinespace
P2. Timing arms in sequential blocks &
Swings above $100\%$ between identical configurations; a $3.7\times$
discrepancy for one arm at one $N$ &
Interleave arms repetition by repetition; report paired ratios \\
\addlinespace
P3. \texttt{synchronize()} with no device argument &
Every kernel at every size reported the event-resolution floor of
$\approx0.4\,\mu$s --- a spectacular speedup &
Pin the device and pass it to every synchronisation \\
\addlinespace
P4. Benchmarking a masked implementation as sparse &
Its aggregation MAC count is unchanged from dense, so the advertised saving is
never executed and the ``sparse'' arm is uniformly slower &
Assert executed FLOPs differ, and that the sparse path issues no
$(B,N,N)\times(B,N,d)$ product \\
\addlinespace
P5. Comparing only against unfused dense &
A $4.6\times$ more favourable comparison at $N{=}512$ than against the
deployed fused kernel &
Report both baselines; check which backend serves the shape and dtype \\
\addlinespace
P6. Trusting the allocator on CPU &
Up to $8.8\times$ inflation, a non-monotone curve, and one cell where the

Pin the heap when timing on CPU; check minor faults per forward \\
\addlinespace
P7. Changing the composition of a timing group &
Two of our own sweeps disagree by $5\times$ on the same nominal cell &
Fix the arm set across sweeps that will be compared, and state it \\

\end{tabular}
\end{table}

P1, P3, P6 and P7 are instrumentation errors: they inflate or flatten a
measurement and they leave traces --- an impossible latency, a non-monotone
curve, an unstable repeat. P2 is a design error that lets a shared environment
leak into a comparison. P4 and P5 are specification errors about \emph{what is
being compared to what}, and they are the dangerous ones, because the resulting
numbers are internally consistent, reproducible and stable under every check
the other five would fail: they answer a different question from the one being
asked. The defence is to state the operation count each arm executes alongside
its latency, which makes a mismatch between the claimed and the executed
algorithm visible immediately.

One belief of our own is worth correcting. We assumed that standard counters
are blind to fused attention kernels, which would make a FLOPs-only comparison
unable to see the baseline that matters. In PyTorch 2.11 that is not so:
\texttt{torch.utils.flop\_counter} reports the fused path correctly, and it was
our harness that added an analytic term on top of the counter's output for that
arm, publishing a figure $1.83\times$ too large. The narrower lesson
generalises better: instrument the counter, then check the
counter. A FLOP model based on $1 - k/(N-1)$ will credit code whose executed
dense aggregation MAC count is unchanged from dense with a saving of $75\%$ at
$N{=}512, k{=}127$, and --- as an analytic property of that formula rather than
a measured run --- with more than $90\%$ wherever $k/(N-1) < 0.1$. Only a
counter attached to the running program will say otherwise.
