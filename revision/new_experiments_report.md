# New experiments

Every measurement added for this revision, what it was for, and what it changed
in the manuscript. All of it is regenerated by
`scripts/run_revision_campaign.sh` on one GPU in under an hour.

A caveat that applies to all of it: both GPUs in this host are shared with other
tenants and carried between 30 and 80 GiB of other processes' allocations and
near-continuous utilisation throughout. Absolute latencies are therefore not
reproducible to better than the drift we report, peak measurements are lower
bounds on what the device can do, and only ratios between implementations timed
in the same interleaved sweep should be compared.

---

## 1. Absolute-k sweep with fused kernels and a matched dense baseline

**Question.** Does a sparse implementation ever beat dense attention, and if so
at what sparsity, once the implementation is no longer the limiting factor?

**Configuration.** Agent counts 12 to 512; retained peers 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32,
64, 128; head width 16; batch 256; `fp32`; seven implementations timed
interleaved, 60 repetitions in each of 3 runs, minimum over samples.

**Script.** `scripts/bench_attention_core.py`
**Artifact.** `results/attention_core_d16.json`

**Result.** Best sparse divided by best dense, at one retained peer: 0.71 at 192
agents, 0.86 at 384, 0.74 at 512. At a quarter of peers: 25.9, 18.4 and 15.3.
The composed PyTorch sparse path never falls below 2.0. At 512 agents and one
retained peer the composed path costs 4.63 ms and the fused one 0.139 ms, a
factor of 33 attributable to implementation.

Agent counts below 192 are excluded from every claim. A full repeat of the sweep
(experiment 9) moves the ratio at 96 agents by a median of 61%, against 0.3% at
192, because those configurations are launch-bound on a contended device. The
first run showed a crossover at 96 agents; the repeat did not, and we report the
restriction rather than the more favourable of the two runs.

**How it changes the manuscript.** It overturns the earlier unconditional claim
that no sparse implementation is faster than dense attention anywhere, and
replaces it with a measured crossover. It is the basis of Section 4.3, Figure 2
and Table 2.

## 2. Backward pass

**Question.** Does the ordering established for the forward pass hold for the
backward pass?

**Configuration.** As above; forward and forward-plus-backward timed under the
same interleaved protocol, for the four differentiable implementations. The
hand-written kernels are forward-only and are excluded.

**Artifact.** `results/attention_core_d16.json`, `backward_total` field.

**Result.** The gathered path's backward costs 0.48 to 0.62 of its own forward
at 192 agents and above, because the retained indices are recorded in the forward
pass and the backward is a scatter of gradients into them; dense attention's
backward costs several times its forward. Against fused dense at a quarter of
peers the ratio falls from 6.0 to 2.2 at 192 agents, 11.0 to 4.1 at 384, and
18.5 to 6.3 at 512, going from forward-only to forward-plus-backward.

**How it changes the manuscript.** New Section 4.6 and Table 4. The conclusion
is unchanged in direction and materially weaker in magnitude, which is the
honest reading and was not previously reported at all.

## 3. Per-stage arithmetic, traffic, intensity and attainment

**Question.** For each stage of each path, how close does it run to whichever
machine limit binds it?

**Configuration.** Agent counts 48 to 512, a quarter of peers retained, batch
256, head width 16. Peak arithmetic and peak bandwidth measured in the same
process with a large matrix multiply and a large copy.

**Script.** `scripts/stage_accounting.py`
**Artifact.** `results/stage_accounting.json`

**Result** (512 agents). The dense aggregation attains 91% of the binding limit
and the sparse aggregation 93%, so neither is on a worse hardware path; the
sparse one is slower because it moves 1.14 GB against 0.29 GB. Top-k selection
attains 9%. The gather attains 84%. Stages whose attainment exceeds 100% are
cache-served and their analytic byte counts are not traffic to memory; all of
them are small.

**How it changes the manuscript.** It refutes an explanation the archival
manuscript gave — that the sparse contraction is slower because a batched
matrix--vector product has lower arithmetic intensity and cannot use the same
hardware paths — and replaces it with a byte criterion, that gathering is
byte-neutral only when the compacted operand is no larger than the score row it
avoids re-reading. Section 4.5, Table 3, Figures 3 and 4.

## 4. End-to-end training-step timing

**Question.** What share of a real training iteration does the communication
module occupy, and can the implementations be told apart end to end?

**Configuration.** One complete MAPPO iteration — 100 rollout steps across 16
environments, then 10 PPO epochs over 4 minibatches — with phase boundaries
separated by device synchronisations, at 6, 12 and 24 agents, for five
communication implementations, five timed iterations after a warm-up.

**Script.** `scripts/bench_training_step.py`
**Artifact.** `results/training_step_timing.json`

**Result.** At 24 agents the environment is 91% of the iteration and the five
implementations span 1.6%, against an iteration-to-iteration spread of 1.7 to
3.6%. The communication-free variant, which performs no attention at all, is not
the fastest of the five.

**How it changes the manuscript.** It replaces an assertion with a measurement
and is reported as a null. Section 4.6, Table 5.

## 5. Fused sparse kernels and a matched fused dense baseline

**Question.** How much of the measured penalty is the composition of
general-purpose operators rather than the algorithm?

**Implementation.** `src/kernels/fused_topk_attention.py` provides a fused
top-k kernel that tiles queries so the score product runs on the
matrix-multiply units, selects by successive argmax rounds, and accumulates the
weighted values without writing a score matrix, an index tensor or a gathered
value tensor; and a variant that selects by an exact bisection on the integer
key of the score, whose cost does not grow with the number retained but which
aggregates densely to achieve that.
`src/kernels/fused_dense_attention.py` provides a streaming dense attention
kernel — the matched-effort baseline.

**Verification.** Every kernel is checked against the reference at every
configuration whose latency is reported. Maximum deviation across the campaign
is at the level of `fp32` rounding. The bisection kernel selects by threshold
rather than by rank, so an exact tie at the k-th score retains one extra peer;
measured incidence is one query row in 196,608 over twelve seeds at the worst
configuration tested.

**Result.** See experiment 1. Separately, the matched dense baseline is
substantially faster than the library fused path at these head widths, and
without it the fused sparse kernel appeared faster than Proposition 1 permits
from sparsity — which is how the unmatched comparison was caught.

**How it changes the manuscript.** Sections 4.1, 4.3 and 7, and a new entry in
the measurement-error table.

## 6. Selecting before scoring

**Question.** Proposition 2 says a pre-score selector escapes the ceiling. Does
the escape appear as time?

**Configuration.** Agent counts 48 to 512, retained peers 2 to 16, batch 256,
head width 16. Two selectors: k nearest peers by Euclidean distance in a
two-dimensional position space, and a Z-order window. Selection, attention over
the retained pairs, and the two together are timed separately.

**Scripts.** `src/kernels/prescore_selection.py`,
`src/kernels/fused_prescore_attention.py`, `scripts/bench_prescore.py`
**Artifact.** `results/prescore.json`

The attention over the retained peers is measured twice: once composed from
library operators (gather keys, gather values, contract, softmax, contract), and
once by a fused kernel that reads one key row and one value row per retained
peer and accumulates with a running maximum, writing nothing but the result. The
second exists because the manuscript's own argument about the score-then-select
path is that a composed constant is an upper bound rather than a property of the
design, and applying that standard to the mechanism the paper criticises but not
to the mechanism it proposes would be incoherent.

**Result** (four retained peers unless stated, every ratio against the better of
the two dense kernels). Composed, the attention stage is slower than dense
attention at every configuration but one, by 0.97 to 5.11 times. Fused, it is
faster at every agent count: 0.78, 0.38, 0.24, 0.11 and 0.09 times dense from 48
to 512 agents, with a best case of 0.055 — eighteen times faster than dense — at
512 agents and two retained peers. Fusing is worth 3.8 to 17.5 times, and the
factor grows with the agent count. End to end the selector decides the outcome.
The Z-order selector costs 0.094 to 0.140 ms across the whole range, so its
share falls as attention grows, and the whole path costs 13.6, 7.5, 3.7, 1.20
and 0.80 times dense from 48 to 512 agents: it crosses below dense attention at
512. The nearest-neighbour selector materialises and ranks an N x N distance
matrix, so its path rises from 6.7 to 29.1 times dense over the same range.

**How it changes the manuscript.** It supplies the escape case the reviewer
asked for, and it is the paper's constructive result: escaping the
operation-count ceiling is necessary and not sufficient, and what decides the
outcome is two things a count cannot see — whether the selector avoids a
quadratic intermediate, and whether the sparse attention left behind is executed
by a kernel written for it. Section 5, Table 7, Figure 4, the abstract, the
introduction and the conclusion.

Two earlier versions of this measurement reached the opposite conclusion and
both were wrong for reasons this paper names elsewhere. The first quoted every
ratio against the vendor library rather than against the better of the two dense
kernels, and reported the Z-order attention stage as roughly twice as fast as
dense; that is the pitfall Section 7 names, and correcting it reversed the sign.
The second, corrected against the right baseline but still composed from library
operators, reported that the attention stage is never faster than dense and
attributed this to the gathered operand having lost the shape the hardware
multiplies quickly. The fused kernel refutes that explanation: over the same
gathered peer sets it is up to eighteen times faster than dense attention, so
what was being measured was the composition and not the shape.

## 8. Corrected machine-balance probe

**Question.** Not a new experiment but a repair. The bandwidth probe allocated
its copy source without writing it, so on the host it read the shared zero page
and reported a bandwidth several times the machine's real figure; and it timed
device work with host wall-clock, which measures launch and synchronisation
rather than the copy.

**Script.** `scripts/roofline.py`
**Artifact.** `results/roofline.json`

**Result.** With both defects fixed the host reports about 223 GB/s
cache-resident and 63 GB/s from memory, against the 1300 GB/s the old probe
reported. Every CPU ridge point derived from the old figure was wrong, and wrong
in the direction that protected the manuscript's argument: against a corrected
ridge the dense path is memory-bound on the host as well as on the GPU, which is
the simpler claim.

**How it changes the manuscript.** The CPU ridge is reported as two
well-defined numbers, cache-resident and memory-resident, instead of a range
that was an artifact of a broken probe. The awkward hedge about using the range
only where a conclusion holds across all of it is gone.

## 9. Tiling search over both kernels

**Question.** Is the fused sparse kernel's advantage a property of sparsity, or
of the two kernels having received unequal engineering?

**Configuration.** Roughly a hundred configurations per kernel — query tile,
key tile or head chunk, warp count, pipeline depth — searched with the same
budget for the dense and the sparse kernel, at agent counts 192 to 512 and head
widths 16 to 128. The dense baseline is the better of that search and the vendor
library. Achieved arithmetic rate is reported as a fraction of the peak the
device attains in the same process.

**Script.** `scripts/autotune_kernels.py`
**Artifact.** `results/autotune.json`

**Result.** The crossover survives tuning and is not produced by an unusually
good sparse kernel: at head widths 16 and 32 the sparse kernel reaches 21–33% of
the device's rate against 30–47% for dense, so it wins only by performing about
half the arithmetic. It is nonetheless confined: dense efficiency climbs to 77%
at head width 128, and the crossover disappears exactly where dense passes about
half the machine.

**How it changes the manuscript.** Section 4.3 and Table 3. The positive result
is now stated as provisional on the dense baseline, in the abstract, the section
and the limitations, and the "matched engineering effort" criterion is replaced
by matched efficiency.

## 10. Full repeat of the headline sweep

**Question.** Does the headline sweep reproduce?

**Configuration.** The identical sweep, repeated on the same device hours later
under different load from other tenants.

**Artifact.** `results/attention_core_d16_repeat.json`

**Result.** From 192 agents upward, median movement between runs is under 1% in
latency and under 0.5% in the ratio, worst configuration under 11%. At 96 agents
every implementation ran between 14% and 394% slower in the second run.

**How it changes the manuscript.** Every claim is restricted to 192 agents and
above. A crossover the first run showed at 96 agents is removed. Appendix A.

## 11. A selection kernel that is flat in the retained count and sparse at once

**Question.** The manuscript previously said that a fused score-then-select
kernel can have sparse aggregation arithmetic *or* a selection cost independent
of the retained count, and that we could not obtain both — while conceding that
a radix or bitonic select would narrow the gap and that we had not written one.
That is a claim about our implementations standing in for a claim about the
design space, and the reviewer asked for it to be tested rather than asserted.

**What was built.** A third fused kernel, `fused_topk_attention_sort`. Scores
are computed through the matrix-multiply units exactly as the reference computes
them, mapped to order-preserving integers by the standard float-key transform,
packed against their own peer index into one 64-bit word, and sorted once per
query row with `tl.sort`. The top k words are gathered, unpacked into scores and
indices, softmaxed, and used to read exactly k value rows. Selection is one
bitonic sort whose depth is logarithmic in the agent count and independent of k;
aggregation touches only the retained rows. Both properties at once, which is
what the manuscript said it could not obtain.

**Configuration.** Agent counts 12 to 512, retained peers 1 to 128, head width
16, batch 256, 60 repetitions interleaved rep-by-rep, three runs, minimum taken.

**Scripts.** `src/kernels/fused_topk_attention.py`,
`scripts/bench_attention_core.py`
**Artifact.** `results/attention_core_sort.json`

**Verification.** Exact against the reference at 123 shapes spanning agent
counts 12 to 512, retained counts 1 to 128 and head widths 16, 64 and 256,
maximum deviation 2.15e-06, and at every configuration in
`scripts/verify_all_configurations.py`. The one class of divergence is an exact
tie at the k-th score, where a rank rule and an index-ordered sort legitimately
retain different peers; the verifier counts the diverging query rows and
classifies the divergence as a tie only when no more rows diverge than have a
tie.

**Result.** The kernel is flat in k as designed: 0.184 to 0.186 ms from one to
sixteen retained peers at 192 agents, against 0.026 to 0.206 ms for the argmax
kernel over the same range. It is also 5.0 times dense attention across that
whole range. It becomes the faster of the two fused sparse kernels only from
sixteen retained peers upward, where both already cost more than five times
dense. At 384 and 512 agents it costs twelve to fifteen times dense at every
sparsity below thirty-two retained peers, because a row-wide sort needs the
whole score row resident and the block of queries that can share a program
shrinks as the row grows.

**How it changes the manuscript.** The claim that we could not obtain both
properties is withdrawn and replaced by a measurement: both can be obtained, and
having both does not help. The sort that buys independence from k costs more
than the argmax rounds it replaces throughout the range in which sparsity would
otherwise pay. Section 4, Table 3.

## 12. A fused kernel for attention over a pre-chosen peer set

**Question.** Section 5 reported that attention over an already-chosen handful
of peers is not cheaper than attention over all of them, and attributed this to
the gathered operand losing the matrix-multiply shape. The reviewer observed
that the measurement composed the stage from library operators — gather keys,
gather values, contract, softmax, contract — which writes and re-reads a
(B, N, k, d) tensor twice, and that the manuscript's own argument elsewhere is
that such a constant is an upper bound rather than a property of the design.

**What was built.** `fused_prescore_attention`, a Triton kernel in which one
program per tile of queries walks that tile's retained peers, reading one key
row and one value row per peer directly out of global memory and accumulating
the softmax-weighted sum with a running maximum. No gathered tensor exists.

**Configuration.** The same grid as experiment 6, so that the fused and composed
columns are the same measurement of the same function.

**Scripts.** `src/kernels/fused_prescore_attention.py`,
`scripts/bench_prescore.py`
**Artifact.** `results/prescore.json`

**Verification.** Exact against the composed path at every configuration the
pre-score artifact reports, both selectors, in
`scripts/verify_all_configurations.py`.

**Result and consequence.** Reported under experiment 6. The negative result was
an artifact of the composition: fused, the same attention is up to eighteen
times faster than the best dense implementation, and the Z-order path crosses
below dense attention end to end at 512 agents. The explanation the manuscript
gave for the negative result is withdrawn.

## Not run, and why

- **Energy.** Both GPUs carry other tenants' work; board-level power would
  attribute their computation to our kernels, and per-process energy accounting
  is unavailable to this account. No energy claim appears in the paper.
- **A second GPU generation.** The host has two cards of the same model.
- **Alternative host allocators.** The allocator effect is already isolated by
  pinning the heap and counting minor faults inside the timed region; swapping
  allocators would establish a fact about allocators, not about attention.
- **A parameter-matched sham-communication variant.** It would sharpen the
  learning study's positive control, which is a precondition of the secondary
  result rather than a result. The budget went to the fused kernels and the
  pre-score selectors instead.
- **Task performance for the pre-score selectors.** They are measured for cost.
  Whether spatial neighbours carry the information attention would have selected
  is the obvious next question and is not answered here, and it matters more now
  that the cost result is positive than it did when it was negative.
- **A learned or hierarchical pre-score selector.** The two we measure bracket
  the outcome — one quadratic, one sub-quadratic — which is what the argument
  needs. Whether a learned selector can be both cheap and useful is a design
  question this paper does not settle.
